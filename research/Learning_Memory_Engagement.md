# DORI Answer Formats: What Research Says About Learning, Memory and Engagement

**Scope.** This report executes **Prompt 1 — Learning science per format** from the DORI research prompt pack, using the supplied format IDs and §0.4 schema. DORI is a self-guided family heritage experience in Mahane Yehuda and Nachlaot, with mixed-age groups, one or more phones, GPS gating, and stops involving inscriptions, markets, murals, architecture and historical photographs. fileciteturn0file0

**Evidence priority.** I prioritized museum, informal-learning and child/family evidence where available, then recent educational studies, and finally classic laboratory findings where field evidence is absent. **OBSERVED** means directly supported by a cited source; **INFERRED** means a design implication from that evidence; **UNKNOWN** means the literature located does not establish the effect for DORI's use case.

## Research synthesis by learning mechanism

### Retrieval, recognition and generation

**OBSERVED — Retrieval practice is useful, but “harder answer format” does not automatically mean “more learning.”** A particularly relevant 2026 field study randomly assigned 149 children aged 4–9 at a children's museum to retrieval practice or restudy. After approximately one week, retrieval practice improved **recognition memory**, but not free recall. This is unusually strong evidence for DORI because it is both child-focused and conducted in an informal museum environment. citeturn802021search0turn802021search1

**INFERRED — This supports asking children to retrieve information during a heritage walk, but does not justify making every question typed recall.** For younger participants, an `ANS-MC1`, `ANS-HOTSPOT`, or other recognition-supported task can still produce a genuine retrieval benefit while reducing failure caused by spelling, working-memory and language demands. The 2026 museum result specifically warns against assuming that successful retrieval practice must culminate in unaided recall. citeturn802021search0

**OBSERVED — Short-answer retrieval is not consistently superior to multiple choice.** A randomized 2024 study comparing very-short-answer questions with four-option MCQs found no significant difference in later retention attributable to the practice format. Initial success was substantially higher for MCQs—73.9% versus 58.6% for very-short answers—which matters because unsuccessful retrieval can reduce the benefit of a nominally more demanding task. The study involved adult students rather than children and was not a field-trip context. citeturn802021search2turn802021search8

**OBSERVED — More sustained retrieval can matter.** A 2025 series of experiments on complex educational concepts found that retrieval practice with feedback could outperform restudy on delayed retention and, under repeated retrieval, on application questions. The effects were not uniformly present after a single exposure. citeturn802021search3

**OBSERVED — Generation can strengthen learning, but only when the learner can successfully generate something useful.** Reviews of generation-based learning find benefits from producing information rather than simply receiving it, including short answer and fill-in-the-blank tasks. Child-focused work also concludes that generation can aid learning when errors are corrected. Yet newer research shows that unguided generation is not universally beneficial: students who generated examples without evaluating their quality showed weaker gains than those who evaluated examples with explanatory feedback. citeturn948097search6turn296603search8turn821788search1

**INFERRED — For DORI, `ANS-SHORT` and `ANS-CLOZE` are strongest when the required answer is readily observable at the place**, such as a word on an inscription, rather than when a six-year-old must retrieve an unfamiliar historical name from memory. Generation is useful when the task creates productive retrieval, not merely keyboard friction.

### Multiple choice, distractors and true/false

**OBSERVED — Multiple choice has both a learning benefit and a misinformation hazard.** Classic experiments found that answering MC questions improved later memory for tested facts, but exposure to plausible wrong alternatives could also cause those distractors to be reproduced as false answers later. Increasing the number of lures increased this effect. citeturn948097search1

**OBSERVED — Corrective feedback greatly reduces that risk.** Butler and Roediger found that both immediate and delayed corrective feedback increased later correct recall and reduced intrusions from MC distractors compared with no feedback. citeturn948097search0turn948097search7

**INFERRED — `ANS-MC1` is therefore a good DORI format when the alternatives themselves provoke observation or discussion, but every factual MC item should reveal the correct answer and ideally explain why after commitment.** A deliberately amusing but historically false distractor should not be allowed to remain the last information a family sees.

**OBSERVED — True/false is not educationally worthless merely because chance performance is 50%.** Four experiments found that true/false practice enhanced later retention compared with rereading, including on short-answer tests. Asking participants to correct statements they marked false improved retention when feedback was supplied. citeturn948097search2

**OBSERVED — However, judging truth can also expose learners to misinformation.** A 2024 three-experiment study found a learning advantage from “truth checking” in only one experiment, while participants became more likely to judge false information as true; the activity also took longer. citeturn948097search5

**INFERRED — `ANS-TF` should therefore be uncommon on a heritage walk.** If used, it is better for confronting a misconception followed immediately by a clear explanation than for ordinary fact recall. “Guess whether this legend is true” without a strong reveal risks making the legend more memorable than the correction.

### Feedback matters more than small differences between formats

**OBSERVED — Feedback has a reliable positive effect on learning.** A 2024 meta-analysis of 116 digitally delivered instructional-feedback interventions found an overall positive effect of approximately **0.41** on learning performance. Even simple correctness feedback outperformed no feedback; process-focused feedback was particularly useful. Both immediate and delayed feedback were effective, with delayed feedback slightly stronger in that dataset, although combinations and contexts varied. citeturn802021search4

**OBSERVED — Guidance for early childhood similarly emphasizes clear, explanatory and timely feedback rather than bare correctness signals.** citeturn296603search9

**INFERRED — For DORI, the important product unit is not just “question → correct/incorrect.” It is “commit to an answer → reveal → concise explanation tied to what is physically visible.”** The explanation should normally come after commitment so that players first retrieve, estimate or reason. A green tick alone wastes much of the educational opportunity.

**INFERRED — Immediate feedback is likely the best default for a walking route**, not because the literature proves it is universally superior, but because a family is physically at the relevant object now. Delaying an explanation until several stops later would separate the corrective information from the physical evidence that can make it meaningful.

### Ordering, estimation and historical reasoning

**OBSERVED — Children's understanding of historical sequence develops earlier than their command of historical dates.** In a study of 58 kindergarten-to-sixth-grade children, even the youngest participants could make meaningful chronological distinctions when arranging historical images. Dates had relatively little meaning before about third grade, and linking dates to historical knowledge became substantially stronger only later. citeturn821788search6turn821788search11

**OBSERVED — Developmental research also finds that forward sequencing of familiar events is easier for young children than backward sequencing or unfamiliar events.** citeturn821788search5

**INFERRED — `ANS-ORDER` is unusually promising for a family heritage walk.** Ordering three visual states—oldest, middle, today—or arranging stages of a visible process may teach chronological structure better than asking a seven-year-old to remember exact dates. It also allows children who cannot type confidently to contribute through reasoning.

**OBSERVED — Historical understanding is broader than chronology.** A widely used historical-reasoning framework emphasizes asking historical questions, using sources, contextualization, argumentation, substantive concepts and meta-concepts. citeturn821788search7

**INFERRED — Ordering should therefore not be treated as proof of causal understanding.** `ANS-ORDER` can support chronology; if the objective is *why* events happened, the ordered task should be followed by a causal prompt or explanation.

**OBSERVED — Numerical estimation develops through childhood and can draw on both visual quantity representations and counting strategies.** A longitudinal study of 350 young children found age-related gains in estimation accuracy and changes in strategy across the transition into formal schooling. citeturn821788search4

**UNKNOWN — I found little direct field evidence showing that estimation questions during museum or heritage walks improve long-term historical learning.**

**INFERRED — `ANS-NUM` is therefore best justified as an observation-and-reasoning mechanic rather than a memory technique:** estimating the number of visible architectural units, approximate age, distance, quantity or change before receiving the actual value. Its educational value should come from comparing intuition with evidence, not from precision scoring.

### Photography, attention and memory

**OBSERVED — Photography can impair memory when it substitutes for looking.** Henkel's museum studies found that participants asked to photograph whole objects later remembered fewer objects, details and locations than participants who simply observed them. Crucially, this “photo-taking impairment effect” disappeared when participants zoomed in on a specific detail; focused photography appeared to engage additional attentional processing. The experiments were conducted during an art-museum tour. citeturn802021search5

**OBSERVED — Photography can also redistribute attention rather than simply reduce memory.** Barasch, Diehl, Silverman and Zauberman found across one field and three laboratory studies that participants allowed to take photographs later recognized more visual information but remembered less auditory information than those who did not photograph. Similar effects appeared when participants merely took “mental photographs,” supporting an attentional explanation. citeturn802021search6

**INFERRED — This strongly supports selective `ANS-PHOTO` use in DORI.** A good photo task tells players *what to notice*: reproduce an archival viewpoint, capture one carved motif, find an example of a visual category, or isolate a detail. A weak photo task merely says “take a picture here.”

**INFERRED — Photo challenges should not coincide with important narration.** If participants are concentrating visually on framing a photograph while audio delivers historical information, Barasch et al.'s findings suggest a plausible competition for attention. Let the visual search happen first and narration or explanation follow.

### Polls, conversation and perspective

**OBSERVED — Family conversation is itself associated with informal learning.** In virtual museum exhibits with 8–9-year-olds and caregivers, both within-exhibit conversation and post-exhibit involvement predicted learning; conversational activity was especially related to productive self-derivation. citeturn296603search2turn296603search3

**OBSERVED — Question form affects how much children say.** In a 2024 children's-museum study of 43 caregiver-child dyads aged 3–6, caregivers mostly asked closed questions. Children whose caregivers predominantly used closed questions produced shorter utterances and had lower measured language outcomes. This is correlational with respect to longer-term language ability, not proof that a single closed question causes poorer learning. citeturn296603search0turn296603search4

**OBSERVED — Prompting can alter family discourse.** A Chicago Children's Museum experiment with 130 families found that brief conversation guidance doubled adults' use of wh-questions, while exhibit-specific instructional prompts increased STEM-related talk. Another museum exhibit study found that prompt design could change the extent to which adults physically took over children's activity. citeturn296603search7turn296603search6

**OBSERVED — Recent museum research also links critical reflection with cultural perspective-taking**, although the 2026 work located here involved adults and undergraduates rather than young family groups. citeturn821788search10

**INFERRED — `ANS-POLL` and `ANS-VOTE` should not be judged by factual-retention standards.** Their best role is to expose different interpretations before a reveal: notice differences, surface assumptions, invite family members to justify a choice, or compare present-day values with historical perspectives. The learning objective is perspective/reflection, not recall.

## Ranked findings for a family heritage walk

**1. Use retrieval, but scaffold it for children — OBSERVED-based.** The strongest directly relevant evidence is the 2026 museum experiment: retrieval improved one-week recognition among 4–9-year-olds even though it did not improve free recall. For DORI, retrieval does not need to mean typing. `ANS-MC1`, `ANS-HOTSPOT`, ordering and well-scaffolded generation can all make learners actively retrieve or discriminate. citeturn802021search0

**2. Treat explanatory feedback as part of the answer format — OBSERVED-based.** Corrective feedback both improves learning and limits misinformation from wrong alternatives. DORI should therefore evaluate “MC + reveal” as a different educational design from “MC + green tick.” citeturn948097search0turn802021search4

**3. Do not assume short answer is educationally superior to multiple choice — OBSERVED-based.** Recent direct comparison found no significant retention advantage, while MC yielded greater initial retrieval success. Choose typed recall only when generating the answer itself serves the objective. citeturn802021search2

**4. Use ordering for historical time, especially with younger children — OBSERVED-based.** Children can reason about chronological pictures before dates become meaningful. For DORI, ordering visual historical states can therefore be more developmentally appropriate than date trivia. citeturn821788search6turn821788search11

**5. Make photography focused rather than documentary — OBSERVED-based.** Whole-object photographing can weaken later memory, whereas zooming into a designated detail avoided that impairment in Henkel's museum study. `ANS-PHOTO` should make the camera an instrument of looking. citeturn802021search5

**6. Separate photo-making from narration — OBSERVED-based.** Photography directs attention toward visual information and can reduce memory for concurrently presented auditory information. A photo challenge should normally precede or follow narration rather than overlap it. citeturn802021search6

**7. Use plausible distractors sparingly and always correct them — OBSERVED-based.** MC distractors can become remembered misinformation. This is especially relevant to heritage content where legends, dates and historical claims may already be easily confused. citeturn948097search1turn948097search0

**8. Use true/false mainly for misconception correction, not routine scoring — OBSERVED-based.** True/false retrieval can aid learning, but merely evaluating false statements can also increase false belief. Asking players to correct a false statement and immediately explaining it is stronger than repeated binary trivia. citeturn948097search2turn948097search5

**9. Design questions to start family conversation rather than terminate it — OBSERVED-based.** Museum studies associate family conversation, open questioning and post-exhibit reflection with richer informal learning. `ANS-POLL`, `ANS-VOTE`, estimates and interpretive MC can create a reason to talk before committing. citeturn296603search2turn296603search7

**10. Match the format to the learning objective rather than optimizing for variety — INFERRED from the evidence.** Recognition formats suit discrimination and noticing; generation formats suit retrievable facts; ordering suits chronology; estimation suits magnitude; polls suit perspective; focused photography suits visual attention; and open family discussion supports inference and reflection. Variety is useful only when these cognitive jobs genuinely differ.

## Negative findings and open questions

**Negative finding — OBSERVED:** More effort is not automatically more learning. A hard short-answer task that produces many retrieval failures can perform no better than a well-designed MC task. citeturn802021search2

**Negative finding — OBSERVED:** Wrong alternatives are not cognitively neutral. Distractors and false statements can become learned unless corrected. citeturn948097search1turn948097search5

**Negative finding — OBSERVED:** Photography is not automatically educational. Unfocused picture-taking can reduce memory for the photographed experience, and visual capture can draw attention away from concurrent audio. citeturn802021search5turn802021search6

**Negative finding — INFERRED:** Formats whose principal difficulty is operating the interface—precise sliders, drag mechanics, typing exact spellings—should not be credited with the benefits of “desirable difficulty” unless that effort is actually tied to retrieval or reasoning.

**Open question — UNKNOWN:** Does focused historical rephotography—such as DORI's proposed “Jerusalem Time Machine”—produce better memory for architectural change and historical context than simply examining a then-and-now pair? Henkel supports focused photography generally, but not this exact heritage mechanic. The cheapest test is a two-condition field pilot with delayed recognition and one open-ended recall question.

**Open question — UNKNOWN:** Does a family vote before revealing an answer increase retention or perspective-taking compared with one person's immediate tap? Museum conversation evidence makes this plausible but does not directly test `ANS-VOTE`. A simple A/B route test could measure conversation time, participation and delayed recall.

**Open question — UNKNOWN:** What level of short-answer difficulty produces productive retrieval rather than discouragement for mixed 6–12-year-old groups? Test `ANS-SHORT` against MC for the same facts, measuring first-attempt success, adult takeover and later memory.

**Open question — UNKNOWN:** Does estimating a visible quantity improve memory for the corrected value or merely increase engagement? Compare estimate-before-reveal with reveal-only for several stops.

**Open question — UNKNOWN:** Does ordering three historical images lead to better causal understanding, or only better chronology? Add one delayed “why did this change?” question to determine whether sequencing transfers beyond temporal order.

**Open question — UNKNOWN:** How frequently can DORI use photo tasks before photography begins to dominate attention and family interaction? Barasch et al. identifies attentional trade-offs but gives no route-level spacing rule. A practical first test is to compare routes with one versus three photo challenges while measuring narration recall and reported enjoyment. citeturn802021search6

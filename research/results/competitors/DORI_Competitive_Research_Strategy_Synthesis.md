# DORI — What We Learned from City Quest Competitors

**Project research note**\
**Prepared:** 4 November 2026\
**Status:** Research synthesis. **No new DORI feature or business model is approved by this document.**\
**Use:** Strategy, content research, and future product discussions. Not an implementation specification.

## 1. Short summary

DORI can aim to offer more than a puzzle played while walking through a city. Its strongest **idea to test** is a game that helps visitors **notice something real, do something with what they notice, and understand the place better**.

The competitor research shows many ways to make city games: repeatable mystery games, place-based discovery, free tourist trails, games for teams, and tools used by teachers or guides. It does **not** yet show that DORI's preferred approach will make people learn more, enjoy the visit more, or pay more. Those outcomes need tests with real players.

Three choices connect almost everything else: **what the player does at the place; how a player continues when stuck; and who pays for the work needed to create and maintain the experience**.

## 3. How competing products work

The same category contains different kinds of businesses. These groups **overlap**.

| Competitor approach | Examples | What is being sold or provided | Key tradeoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| Repeatable mystery entertainment | Eskap | A story-led team game offered in many cities | Easier to repeat, but may use fewer unique details of each place |
| Games made by local creators | Questo, Secret City Trails | Paid routes, sometimes through a marketplace | More local voices, but content quality and support vary |
| Tools for people who make games | Explorama, Actionbound | Software, publishing access and/or licenses | Scales through creators; a tool alone does not guarantee good puzzles |
| Tourism and heritage discovery | Baludik, Tèrra Aventura | Experiences supported by destinations or local partners | Can be free to visitors; depends on institutional support and upkeep |
| Group and event activities | Goosechase, Urban Quest, CityDays | Team play, events, and/or organizer tools | Strong group support may add setup, time limits or devices |
| Local Jerusalem games | Mifrasim, BeeTravel | Location-specific games and activities | Most directly relevant to Amazing Shuk, but their hidden challenge content is not yet understood |

**Important:** These are research categories, not official company labels. We should compare products within the right buying context: one family ticket cannot be compared directly with an annual school license.

## 4. The whole DORI system

A good route depends on three connected parts.

**A. The visitor experience**\
Find the game → understand who it is for and what it costs → start easily → walk to a place → notice a real clue → act or answer → get help if needed → see a short explanation → choose or reach the next stop → finish and reflect.

**B. The content and upkeep work**\
Research the place → record sources and different kinds of claims → write a small activity and explanation → check language, accessibility and permissions → test it on site → publish → watch for failed clues → update or retire content.

**C. The business and access model**\
Choose the buyer (visitor, guide, school, tourism body) → decide who and how many people can play → make payment and access clear → provide needed support → earn enough to cover production and ongoing upkeep.

These parts affect one another. More detailed local content may deepen the experience, but costs more to research and maintain. Multi-phone teamwork can create new roles, but affects price and access. Open routes can help with closed shops, but make story order harder to control.

## 5. Eight findings and what they mean for DORI

Each finding below separates **what competitors show**, **what we infer**, and **what needs testing**.

### 1. A real place should matter to the challenge

- **Seen:** Eskap sells repeatable missions in many cities; one independent review criticized limited use of local surroundings. Explorama describes an action followed by a game and explanation. Adventure Lab's published remote demo substitutes pictures for clues normally found on site.
- **Meaning:** A strong task may ask visitors to look closely at something they could not easily observe from elsewhere. This is a **design idea**, not proven superior learning.
- **Choices:** A fully local puzzle; a repeatable story with locally written tasks; or a heritage walk with optional games.
- **Risk:** Signs move, vendors change, and physical details may be hard for some players to see.
- **Test:** Try the same three tasks with visitors **on site** and people **away from the site**. Record what they can solve, what they notice and what they remember.

### 2. Clear history and honest storytelling could be a strength

- **Seen:** Games use real history, legends and fictional stories, but the public materials studied rarely show exactly how these are labelled inside a paid experience. This is **missing evidence**, not proof that competitors do not label them.
- **Meaning:** DORI could clearly distinguish a documented fact, someone's memory, a legend, a fictional story and an interpretation—without turning the screen into a textbook.
- **Choices:** A short label near the reveal; a narrator who explains what is known; or an optional source panel.
- **Risk:** Too many labels can interrupt the story; a confident-sounding explanation may go beyond the evidence.
- **Test:** Show equal-length explanations with and without simple labels. Ask what players believe, how sure they feel, and what they remember later.

### 3. Getting stuck is part of the game design

- **Seen:** Tèrra Aventura uses limited help and jokers; Actionbound supports configurable hints and answer handling; some games use time costs or one attempt. Exact rules differ and some reports conflict.
- **Meaning:** For a self-guided game, players should have a way to continue without needing a live guide. Failing a riddle should not block the educational explanation.
- **Choices:** Free hints; hints with a disclosed point cost; or a clear **show answer and continue** action.
- **Risk:** Too much help makes the activity feel empty; harsh penalties punish families and different reading speeds.
- **Test:** Compare simple hints, costed hints and direct reveal. Measure stuck moments, completion and later understanding.

### 4. Route freedom, branching stories and story order are different decisions

- **Seen:** Specific Baludik routes allow stops in any order and save progress. Story City advertises branches that change the route or ending. An inaccessible stage can block a strictly ordered route.
- **Meaning:** **Open order** means the same stops in a different order. **Branching** means player choices change which stops or story parts they get. The second is harder to write and maintain.
- **Choices:** Fixed story spine plus optional stops; flexible groups of stops; or true choice-driven branches.
- **Risk:** Spoilers, longer walks, more writing, unequal scores between branches and harder recovery.
- **Test:** Compare a fixed three-stop route with a flexible route. Test branching separately on paper; do not assume it improves learning or fun.

### 5. Team design and pricing start with the number of phones

- **Seen:** Eskap requires one phone per player; Mifrasim advertises one phone for a group; Questo has phone bundles and shared progress. Some games do not support solo players.
- **Meaning:** Device rules affect who can join, what they pay, and whether every person has a meaningful role.
- **Choices:** One phone with shared group roles; optional second-phone clues; or one phone per player. Always consider a solo path if the product is meant to serve solo visitors.
- **Risk:** Higher costs, lost batteries, one person controlling a shared screen, and excluding children without devices.
- **Test:** Watch real families play one task with one phone and with two phones. Compare participation and comprehension; test price offers separately.

### 6. Content upkeep is part of product quality

- **Seen:** Explorama documents testing and mission performance checks. Other sources describe routes under maintenance, hidden clues and language errors.
- **Meaning:** A place-based game needs a repeatable process for keeping clues, stories and media correct. More local content often means more upkeep.
- **Choices:** Focus on stable architecture; offer fallback tasks; gather player reports; and schedule on-site checks.
- **Risk:** Quietly broken challenges, old translations, unreviewed historical claims and high running costs.
- **Test:** Follow three Mahane Yehuda/Nachlaot stops over three months; record changes and how long fixes take.

### 7. DORI may need more than one way to make money

- **Seen:** Some competitors charge per person, per device or per team. Others sell publishing tools or licenses to organizers. Some destination games are free to players and supported by public bodies.
- **Meaning:** The **player**, **payer** and **organizer** may be different people. A school, guide and tourist family may use related content but need different access and support.
- **Choices:** Consumer passes; guide or school packages; commissioned destination experiences; or a mix.
- **Risk:** Content costs can exceed ticket income. Public contracts can be slow and may change editorial goals. No DORI price or margin is yet proven.
- **Test:** Ask each buyer type what they need, show simple offers, and test real interest. Investigate Israeli destination funding rather than assuming the French model transfers.

### 8. Local competition, food tasks and new story forms need direct study

- **Seen:** Mifrasim and BeeTravel already advertise games around Mahane Yehuda. Food often appears as a theme, coupon or paid offer. Fiction-based branching already exists; **branching through different views of real history** was not established in the sample.
- **Meaning:** DORI cannot claim to be the first market game, first branching game or only family-friendly game. Food-based and multiple-perspective activities may be promising, but their real value and distinctiveness are unknown.
- **Choices:** A food observation task without buying; an optional tasting with a clear alternative; a choice of whose account to explore without inventing history.
- **Risk:** Food allergies, shop hours, vendor consent, purchase pressure, turning people into attractions, and oversimplifying sensitive history.
- **Test:** Inspect local competitors' actual experiences first. Then try one no-purchase food activity and one small multiple-perspective story test with participants.

## 6. What is a real opportunity, and what is still unknown?

| Possibility | What we can say now |
|---|---|
| Place-based task plus a sourced explanation | **Promising combination** based on examples and counterexamples; player value is not proven |
| Self-guided consumer game with an optional guide layer | **Possible market position** with some support; not proven empty of competitors |
| Clear fact / legend / fiction labels | **Not visible in available evidence**; requires paid-game content review |
| English-language game focused on Mahane Yehuda | **Unconfirmed**; needs a full catalogue and local search |
| Choice-driven routes | **Already exist** in general; not a unique claim |
| Solo, family and team modes | **Already exist** in competing products; execution may still differ |
| Story choices between real viewpoints | **Not established in the sample**; not a proven market gap |
| Free-to-player games funded by Israeli public bodies | **Unresearched business option** |
| No-purchase sensory or food challenge | **Not found in the sample**; hidden gameplay may contain examples |

**Rule:** “We did not find it” is not the same as “nobody does it.”

## 7. What to investigate next — suggested order

These are **research priorities**, not an approved build plan.

| Order | Question | Cheapest useful next step | What decision it helps |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Does the activity really depend on the place? | Three-stop paper prototype, remote versus on-site | Core learning experience |
| 2 | What do local and Jaffa competitors actually show players? | Authorized play of Mifrasim and Questo; short observation notes | Differentiate from real alternatives |
| 3 | Do brief evidence labels help or hurt understanding? | Compare two short reveal versions with players | Historical/editorial treatment |
| 4 | Can people finish without outside help? | Hint ladder versus direct reveal | Self-guided experience |
| 5 | Does one phone or two work better for families? | Short family observation test | Social experience and access |
| 6 | What changes on the route over time? | Three-month clue check | Content maintenance needs |
| 7 | Is there unmet demand for an English market game? | Local catalogue review in English/Hebrew | Positioning and launch audience |
| 8 | Who could pay for DORI? | Interviews with families, guides, schools and local bodies | Price and revenue model |
| 9 | Do food tasks work without a required purchase? | One vendor-approved observation prototype | Ethical sensory activities |
| 10 | Does story branching help? | Compare fixed, fictional branch and perspective-choice paper stories | Long-term story direction |

For each test, define **what to measure**, **what result would change a decision**, and **what would count as a failure** before running it. Small tests can guide the next test; they do not prove that the same results hold for all visitors.

## 8. Research quality and remaining gaps

**What is reasonably documented**
- Published game formats, device rules, advertised route properties, many purchase and licensing models, and some creator tools.
- Several useful negative cases: weak use of place, blocked physical clues, one-answer limits and phone-heavy experiences.
- Differences between open-order routes and true branching choices.

**What remains weak**
- Actual paid challenge sequences, hint screens, post-task explanations and accessibility alternatives.
- Whether the reported local games work well for tourists and families.
- How much people understand, enjoy or remember after play.
- Willingness to pay, route profit, customer acquisition cost and repeat institutional buying.
- Whether DORI's ideas are genuinely rare or simply hidden inside paid games.

**Audit of the combined material**
- Claude's later library is the broader source of comparison data: it contains 18 strategy profiles and a 22-entity comparison; the workbook audit also counts wider platform records and leads. These are **different counting scopes**, not an error to smooth over.
- Its Phase 2 summary reports 361 sourced cells out of 929 applicable cells, about **39% coverage**. Most are documentation, not tested play.
- The earlier Phase 4 memo used 14 platforms, eight adventures, 70 sources and one partial challenge record. Those records largely overlap with the later library.
- Both studies agree: **no complete paid challenge was observed, no DORI field evaluation was conducted, and no measured learning advantage was shown**.
- The later library provides wider competitive and strategic coverage; the earlier memo's three-part system model and its separation of learning, enjoyment and commercial value are retained here.

## 9. Rules for using this document

1. Use this as a **research note**, not as approved DORI requirements.
2. Keep a clear distinction between **company documentation**, **public demo**, **player/reviewer report**, **field observation**, and **our inference**.
3. Before using a price, language option, game route or business claim in a decision, check its current source and exact purchase unit.
4. Don't copy paid puzzles or imply that anyone played a game when they did not.
5. For history, memory and people: use project-approved sources, show uncertainty when needed, and avoid reducing communities to fixed categories.
6. Keep **educational effect**, **player experience** and **business success** as separate things to measure.

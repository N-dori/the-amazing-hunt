# The Art of Turning Guided Tours into Interactive Narrative Adventures

> **Central principle:** Technology should give visitors a reason to look more closely at the world, ask better questions, interact with other people, and experience discoveries they could not easily have made on their own.

**Prepared:** 8 November 2026  
**Audience:** Independent tour guides, small operators, heritage educators, and experience designers  
**Scope:** Human-led guided tours of approximately 2–8 hours  
**Evidence convention:** **[Research]** peer-reviewed study; **[Implemented]** documented real-world example; **[Proposal]** original, not yet validated tour design; **[Judgment]** practical assessment rather than measured effectiveness.

## 1. Executive summary: Seven findings

The future of guided tours is not simply more information delivered through phones. It is **real-world places becoming interactive stories that visitors help uncover**. A building can become a clue, a street a historical contradiction, a landscape a scientific puzzle, and a marketplace the setting for a consequential decision.

1. **Narrative integration is more important than technological intensity.** **[Research]** Ballina's 2026 Oviedo Cathedral Tower field quasi-experiment compared three guided-tour formats involving 135 paying visitors. A smoothly integrated, QR-triggered experience outperformed both a conventional tour and a less integrated tablet-AR experience across many reported outcomes. The single-site and measurement limitations matter. [R1]
2. **Curiosity starts with an unanswered question.** **[Design inference]** Let visitors form theories and seek clues before the guide reveals the answer. Good mysteries are fair, observable, and resolvable with authentic evidence.
3. **The human guide remains central.** **[Research]** Narrative-based, multisensory guiding in Hong Kong's Yim Tin Tsai was associated with enriched visitor impressions and emotional connection. [R3]
4. **The visitor can become an investigator or collaborator.** **[Implemented]** Commercial platforms such as Secret City Trails and Questo use real streets and location-specific puzzles. A human-led guide can borrow the narrative mechanics without surrendering the tour to an app. [R8–R9]
5. **Conversational AI enables exploration, but not automatic factual improvement.** **[Research]** A 2026 ARtour study found comparable task performance to web search but more exploratory information seeking with an AI conversational interface. Historical grounding and governance remain concerns. [R2]
6. **Simple sensory and physical interactions may outperform flashy screens.** **[Judgment]** A twenty-second soundscape, archival print, role card, or observation challenge can be better integrated than a complex headset experience.
7. **The first pilot can be small.** **[Proposal]** Three stops, one central mystery, two observation challenges, and one final reveal are sufficient to test whether narrative redesign improves participation.

**Decision rule:** Add technology only when it reveals otherwise inaccessible evidence, supports meaningful choice, improves accessibility, or allows a useful form of interaction. Otherwise, keep the moment human-led.

## 2. The science of curiosity, storytelling, and discovery

Narrative activities are attractive because they engage **anticipation**, **interpretation**, **emotion**, **social discussion**, and **memory**. These mechanisms are not the same as proven long-term learning gains, and evidence from classrooms, museums, games, and XR environments cannot automatically be transferred to outdoor tours.

| Narrative technique | Why it may work | Physical-tour translation | Human guide's role | Useful technology | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mystery | A question creates a reason to keep investigating | A vanished building's history is reconstructed over four stops | Set stakes, offer fair hints, resolve evidence | QR clue cards | Ambiguity can become frustration |
| Progressive revelation | Each clue changes interpretation | Reveal one new historical source at each landmark | Maintain continuity | Short mobile pages | Too many reveals fragment attention |
| Foreshadowing/payoff | An early detail becomes meaningful later | Introduce a symbol at stop 1, explain it at stop 5 | Recall earlier observations | Photo zoom-in | Avoid artificial coincidences |
| Competing perspectives | Disagreement invites source criticism | Compare official, personal, and material evidence | Label speculation and provenance | Audio/text witness cards | Never invent authentic quotations |
| Character-centered narrative | A person makes complex history relatable | Follow a documented resident's changing fortunes | Provide historical context and empathy | Curated character dialogue | Fiction must be disclosed |
| Meaningful choices | Decisions increase agency | Debate a historical choice and its constraints | Facilitate respectful disagreement | Live poll | Choices must not trivialize harm |
| Environmental storytelling | Surroundings become evidence | Observe patched stone, worn steps, or altered watercourses | Teach close looking | Then/now overlay | Avoid unsupported interpretation |
| Collaborative mystery | Different visitors contribute | Assign complementary evidence cards | Balance voices, synthesize | Shared answer board | Prevent dominant players taking over |
| Final resolution | Earlier moments form a coherent whole | Group reconstructs a historical change | Connect facts and reflection | Optional digital souvenir | Avoid a forced twist unsupported by history |

**Research interpretation.** The best direct evidence here concerns integrated guided interpretation and visitor-reported experience [R1, R3]. A conversational heritage interface provides adjacent evidence about exploratory behavior [R2]. They **do not** establish that every mystery structure improves delayed memory or learning.

## 5. Nine structures for guided tours

1. **Detective investigation.** Start with a questionable claim about a building; use physical details to test it.
2. **Historical mystery.** Explain the disappearance or transformation of a site through evidence distributed over several stops.
3. **Time-travel journey.** Travel through historical periods while remaining in one physical district.
4. **Quest or mission.** Trace an artifact, trade route, lost document, or ecological process.
5. **Character-centered journey.** Follow the documented experiences of one resident or clearly identified composite character.
6. **Competing perspectives.** Study one event from several sourced viewpoints and material evidence.
7. **Scientific discovery.** Form predictions, gather environmental observations, and refine a model.
8. **Environmental mystery.** Explain an unexpected landscape pattern, a changed stream, or an unusual species distribution.
9. **Collaborative adventure.** Give teams different evidence so that a solution requires discussion.

**Continuity rule:** For a 3–4-hour tour, choose *one primary arc*. Each stop should answer a small question while deepening the larger question. Walking time can serve as anticipation; breaks should not contain essential clues.

## 6. Narrative design for different ages

| Group | Story structure | Participation | Facilitator adaptation | Technology |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ages 4–7 | Concrete treasure or character quest | Find shapes, sounds, colors, and objects | Brief instructions, movement, repeated motif | Optional images; caregiver holds device |
| Ages 8–12 | Detective/expedition | Small teams and tangible clues | Give hints and roles | Short QR reveals |
| Ages 13–17 | Contested decisions, investigation | Autonomy, comparison, debate | Avoid patronizing competition | Polls and selected archives |
| Young adults | Multi-layered mystery | Social choice and optional deeper evidence | Invite critical interpretations | Optional adaptive paths |
| Adults | Historical causes and dilemmas | Reasoned comparison and reflection | Connect personal and structural history | Archival maps/text |
| Older adults | Human narratives and transformations | Conversation, memories, comparison | Flexible pace and accessible text | Optional larger text/audio |
| Multigenerational | Complementary clue quest | Each age contributes a different element | Keep all roles equally valuable | One shared device is enough |

**Same place, six versions:** At a historical gate, young children search for a symbol; older children investigate gatekeeping; teenagers debate opening it during a crisis; adults examine trade and authority; older visitors compare archival memories; mixed-age families receive complementary evidence. All versions use the same verified site history.

| Technology | Narrative capability | Independent-guide fit | Visitor requirements | Known considerations |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| **ThingLink** | Hotspots on historic images or 360° views | Good for archival tours | Browser in typical use | Subscription and bandwidth considerations |
| **QR + static website** | Single reveal, source card, or clue | Excellent | Browser; connectivity unless cached | Test scan access and link permanence |
| **AI writing assistant** | Draft clues, audience variants, translations | Excellent behind the scenes | None if guide pre-prepares content | Human verification of every historical claim |
| **Source-grounded AI characters** | Responsive inquiry from a persona | Experimental | Device, connectivity, language interface | Hallucination, disclosure, moderation, provenance |
| **Historic image comparison** | Past-versus-present reveal | Excellent | Optional shared screen or paper | Image rights, viewpoint alignment |
| **Phone-based AR** | Visualize lost structures in context | Medium to difficult | Compatible device, lighting, positioning | Tracking drift, battery, high production effort |
| **Digital map/location triggers** | Spatial narrative episodes | Medium | GPS/connection depending on tool | Urban canyons, indoor drift, accessibility |

**Preferred stack:** Start with QR-linked HTML/Twine pages, curated historical images, a group poll, and printed backups. Use AI primarily to help the guide author, test, translate, and adapt the narrative—not to invent public-facing historical facts.

## 9. Twelve original activities

Each activity below is an **original proposal**, not a proven commercial product. Time and effort are approximate; software and printing budgets exclude the guide's labor, research permissions, and custom development.

### 9.1 The clue nobody noticed
- **Hook:** “One detail proves that this building used to serve another purpose.”
- **Best for:** Historic city tours; ages 8+; **4 minutes**.
- **Visitor action:** Examine a façade in pairs, identify a feature, defend a hypothesis.
- **Guide:** Gives context, hints, and evidence-based explanation.
- **Technology:** Optional QR close-up; mobile browser; no app; connectivity optional with paper print.
- **Build:** Photograph the feature, write one question and two graduated hints; **1–2 hours**, minimal direct cost.
- **Evaluation:** Record observations and correct evidence-based explanations.
- **Accessibility:** Provide high-resolution printed image and spoken description.
- **Mechanism:** Attention and deduction; evidence is adjacent rather than a validation of this exact activity.

### 9.2 The historical detective
- **Hook:** “Why was this district transformed?”
- **Best for:** City/heritage; ages 10+; **6 minutes at three stops**.
- **Action:** Teams gather archival and physical clues across multiple landmarks.
- **Guide:** Regulates difficulty, rejects unsupported claims, delivers final synthesis.
- **Technology:** QR clue pages or Twine; browser, optional connectivity after local preloading.
- **Build:** Research 3–4 sources and prepare clue cards; **5–8 hours**, low direct cost.
- **Evaluation:** Can groups cite at least two pieces of evidence in their conclusion?
- **Fallback:** Printed envelopes; adjustable clues for multilingual teams.

### 9.3 The missing artifact
- **Hook:** “How did this object arrive here?”
- **Best for:** Archaeology, trade, cultural heritage; families/adults; **5–8 minutes**.
- **Action:** Reconstruct an artifact's provenance, manufacture, and circulation using complementary evidence.
- **Guide:** Ensures provenance is documented and counters sensational archaeology.
- **Technology:** Interactive chronology or image cards, mobile browser optional.
- **Build:** Select sourced artifact and 4–6 evidence fragments; **4–6 hours**.
- **Evaluation:** Count valid causal links; fallback printed timeline.

### 9.4 The competing witnesses
- **Hook:** “Three accounts describe the same event. Which claims survive scrutiny?”
- **Best for:** Historical/cultural tours; teenagers/adults; **8 minutes**.
- **Action:** Compare official account, personal testimony, and physical record.
- **Guide:** Distinguishes corroborated from speculative material.
- **Technology:** Short audio or text snippets; browser optional; offline transcript available.
- **Build:** Verify and excerpt sources; **6–10 hours**.
- **Evaluation:** Quality of source-based reasoning; accessible transcripts and readable print.

### 9.5 The time-travel decision
- **Hook:** “You are responsible for this town at a moment of crisis. What do you do?”
- **Best for:** History; ages 10+; **5 minutes**.
- **Action:** Vote, justify choice, hear consequences and real historical outcome.
- **Guide:** Explains period constraints and avoids judging modern visitors simplistically.
- **Technology:** Mentimeter or show of hands; browser if digital.
- **Build:** Research options and a reveal; **2–4 hours**.
- **Evaluation:** Participation and reasoning, not agreement with guide.

### 9.6 The city's secret
- **Hook:** “Why does the street suddenly become narrower here?”
- **Best for:** Urban history; ages 12+; **5–7 minutes**.
- **Action:** Infer hidden infrastructure from buildings, levels, or street geometry.
- **Guide:** Reveals documented physical/historical explanation.
- **Technology:** Archival map comparison on browser; optional printed map.
- **Build:** Secure map/images and write the reveal; **4–8 hours**.
- **Evaluation:** Recognition of similar clues later.

### 9.7 The environmental investigator
- **Hook:** “Why does this plant grow here but not a few meters away?”
- **Best for:** Nature/field trips; ages 7+; **8 minutes**.
- **Action:** Predict, observe soil/light/water patterns, and discuss alternatives.
- **Guide:** Models scientific caution and safety.
- **Technology:** Optional identification reference, never treated as conclusive.
- **Build:** 1-page observation sheet and source notes; **2–5 hours**.
- **Evaluation:** Number of specific observations and justified explanations.
- **Fallback:** Paper notebook; no collecting protected species.

### 9.8 The forgotten person's story
- **Hook:** “What does this street look like through one person's life?”
- **Best for:** Social heritage; ages 12+; **6 minutes at selected stops**.
- **Action:** Piece together life events from archival traces and context.
- **Guide:** Keeps individuals from becoming caricatures.
- **Technology:** Brief audio or curated text; AI only for reviewed drafting/translation.
- **Build:** Research and edit 3–5 episodes; **6–12 hours**.
- **Evaluation:** Recall and empathy with contextual accuracy.
- **Fallback:** Illustrated cards and oral storytelling.

### 9.9 The impossible choice
- **Hook:** “Every option has a cost.”
- **Best for:** Conflict, civic, cultural history; teenagers/adults; **6 minutes**.
- **Action:** Groups debate historically plausible alternatives.
- **Guide:** Explains the actual decision and who was affected.
- **Technology:** Group vote optional.
- **Build:** Source alternative options; **2–4 hours**.
- **Evaluation:** Breadth of reasons and respectful exchange.
- **Fallback:** Colored voting cards.

### 9.10 The collaborative mystery
- **Hook:** “No one has enough evidence to solve it alone.”
- **Best for:** Families, teams; **8–10 minutes**.
- **Action:** Each sub-group receives a distinct clue, then teams compare findings.
- **Guide:** Facilitates inclusion and final synthesis.
- **Technology:** Shared answer board optional; one phone per group sufficient.
- **Build:** Prepare three interlocking clue sets; **5–8 hours**.
- **Evaluation:** Contribution distribution and quality of solution.
- **Fallback:** Printed cards.

### 9.11 The location-based revelation
- **Hook:** “The secret becomes visible from this viewpoint.”
- **Best for:** Architecture, landscape, urban tours; ages 8+; **3–5 minutes**.
- **Action:** Locate the correct safe viewpoint and interpret a previously unseen feature.
- **Guide:** Keeps group safe and explains interpretation.
- **Technology:** QR image reveal; GPS not necessary.
- **Build:** One page and position-specific source image; **3–6 hours**.
- **Evaluation:** Number of visitors noticing the detail before reveal.
- **Fallback:** Printed comparison photo.

### 9.12 The final discovery
- **Hook:** “What was the place really telling us?”
- **Best for:** Any tour; all ages; **5–8 minutes**.
- **Action:** Reassemble clues, explain the finding, reflect on changed perception.
- **Guide:** Gives satisfying, evidence-grounded resolution.
- **Technology:** Shared digital summary or optional souvenir page.
- **Build:** Create one reusable summary format; **2–4 hours**.
- **Evaluation:** Multi-stop recall and ability to describe causal connections.
- **Fallback:** Oral reflection circle.

## 10. Three complete tour blueprints

These are **hypothetical routes**, not claims about a particular destination. Align locations and episodes with actual evidence before selling a tour.

### Tour A: The City as a Mystery — 3 hours

**Question:** Why did an important district decline or change?  
**Narrative arc:** Apparent prosperity → conflicting physical clues → trade and social change → contradictory testimony → revised explanation.  
**Audience:** Families with older children, teenagers, adults.

| Time | Episode | Human guide | Visitor action | Digital layer | Return to reality |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 00:00–00:25 | Opening contradiction | Introduces historical puzzle | Offer hypotheses | QR evidence card | Look at actual street |
| 00:25–00:55 | Oldest building | Explains architectural context | Find reused features | Archival overlay | Point to features |
| 00:55–01:25 | Market routes | Connects economy and place | Infer trade movement | Historic map | Trace real routes |
| 01:25–01:40 | Break | Informal conversation | Share theories | None | Rest |
| 01:40–02:10 | Conflicting witnesses | Facilitates source criticism | Compare two accounts | Short audio/text | Examine site |
| 02:10–02:40 | Decisive clue | Guides discovery | Find hidden pattern | Final clue screen | Verify on buildings |
| 02:40–03:00 | Resolution | Synthesizes supported explanation | State group conclusion | Optional souvenir | Final reflection |

**Three revelations:** The opening claim is incomplete; witness accounts conflict; an overlooked physical change provides a better explanation.  
**Low-cost MVP:** Three QR pages, a map, one vote, paper backups.  
**Measure:** Evidence-based explanations, spontaneous questions, guide workload.

### Tour B: The Time-Travel Journey — 4 hours

**Question:** How would life change if you inhabited the same place in three different eras?  
**Arc:** Arrival → everyday life → trade and contact → crisis → transformation → modern legacy.  
**Audience:** Cultural travelers, teenagers, adults.

| Time | Episode | Guide-led interpretation | Participation | Technology |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 00:00–00:35 | Earliest era | Introduce settlement | Find oldest physical traces | Evidence-labeled reconstruction |
| 00:35–01:10 | Everyday life | Present documented resident roles | Choose a role and infer constraints | Curated dialogue/image |
| 01:10–01:45 | Culture and trade | Explain objects and movement | Infer origins of materials | Interactive object cards |
| 01:45–02:05 | Break | Pause | Share observations | None |
| 02:05–02:40 | Crisis | Explain historical stakes | Vote on an impossible choice | Browser poll |
| 02:40–03:15 | Changed city | Interpret surviving traces | Match old/new viewpoints | Archival gallery |
| 03:15–03:45 | Legacy | Connect past and present | Identify lasting consequences | Short reveal |
| 03:45–04:00 | Return | Lead reflection | Describe changed view of place | Optional souvenir |

**Surprises:** Ordinary life differs from stereotypes; a choice has unintended consequences; contemporary street layout preserves past decisions.  
**Safety/authenticity:** Review reconstructions; disclose composite characters; provide all dialogue in text.  
**MVP:** A role card, decision, and two historical image pairs.

### Tour C: The Hidden World — 4 hours

**Question:** What unseen ecological or historical systems explain this landscape?  
**Arc:** Puzzle → field evidence → biological pattern → causal hypothesis → human influence → verification.  
**Audience:** Families, school groups, nature enthusiasts.

| Time | Episode | Guide role | Visitor action | Digital layer / fallback |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 00:00–00:30 | Landscape puzzle | Frame observation | Predict differences | Optional poll / hands |
| 00:30–01:05 | Ground evidence | Interpret soil and rock safely | Describe patterns | Print observation card |
| 01:05–01:40 | Living clues | Guide species observation | Compare habitats | Optional ID reference |
| 01:40–02:00 | Break | Rest and recap | Share hypotheses | None |
| 02:00–02:35 | Invisible system | Explain relationships | Model water/soil/vegetation | Diagram / print |
| 02:35–03:10 | Human influence | Discuss land-use history | Find physical traces | Old landscape photos |
| 03:10–03:40 | Test the model | Support prediction | Test hypothesis at a new site | Observation sheet |
| 03:40–04:00 | Resolution | Connect observations | Explain system and uncertainty | Optional group summary |

**Surprises:** Similar sites behave differently; an ordinary feature reveals a system; the apparently untouched landscape contains human history.  
**MVP:** Paper observation sheets and annotated photos; fully offline.  
**Safety:** No required GPS, phones only when stationary, no off-trail clue-hunting.

## 11. A practical narrative design toolkit

### Ten-step method

1. Inventory **authentic** stories, physical evidence, characters, conflicts, and uncertainties along an existing route.
2. Select one compelling central question.
3. Choose the dominant structure (mystery, time travel, quest, scientific discovery, character journey).
4. Outline 5–7 episodes mapped to places already visited.
5. Design one observable clue or question per key stop.
6. Add at least two moments of conversation or shared choice.
7. Introduce technology only where it provides a distinctive benefit.
8. Adapt complexity, reading load, and participation for the group.
9. Prototype 2–3 moments and prepare accessible non-digital equivalents.
10. Pilot, observe, ask for feedback, and revise.

### Copy-ready AI prompts

**Story discovery prompt**

> Analyze these tour stops and verified historical facts. Identify five possible central mysteries, contradictions, or human dilemmas. For each, point to physical evidence visitors can observe. Separate verified information, interpretation, and unsupported ideas. Never invent historical sources.

**Five-minute episode prompt**

> Using only the evidence below, design a five-minute outdoor activity with an opening hook, visible clue, visitor hypothesis, optional hint, and evidence-based revelation. Include adaptations for ages 8–12, teens, and adults; include a paper fallback and accessibility accommodations.

**Narrative quality review prompt**

> Review this tour episode for narrative coherence, historical accuracy, fairness of clues, unwanted screen time, accessibility, privacy, visitor safety, and guide workload. Provide specific revisions and label evidence gaps.

## 13. A 30-day pilot roadmap

### Week 1 — Story design
- [ ] Select an existing route and an authentic unresolved question.
- [ ] Verify evidence for three stops using reliable archives, site guides, or specialists.
- [ ] Decide the narrative hook, climax, and final explanation.
- [ ] Pick success indicators before building.

### Week 2 — Create the MVP
- [ ] Build two short QR/HTML interactions and one final reveal.
- [ ] Include authentic images and visible source notes.
- [ ] Draft guide lines and clear handoff cues.
- [ ] Prepare laminated backup cards and multilingual text where necessary.

### Week 3 — Field test
- [ ] Test in the same weather/light/connection conditions as the tour where practical.
- [ ] Check scan time, readability, battery impact, and accessibility.
- [ ] Rehearse every transition and an entirely offline version.
- [ ] Revise clues that are too difficult, ambiguous, or slow.

### Week 4 — Pilot
- [ ] Run an enhanced guided tour with real visitors and informed feedback collection.
- [ ] Where possible, compare with a similarly structured conventional tour.
- [ ] Count contributions, questions, task completion, troubleshooting, and delays.
- [ ] Request brief post-tour and delayed recall feedback.
- [ ] Keep only digital elements that clearly earn their place.

**Illustrative direct-cost budgets, not vendor quotations:** lean $0–$50; polished no-code $50–$300; advanced/custom AR separately scoped. These exclude the guide's labor and historical research. Do not equate a small pilot with statistically established causal effects.

## 14. Evaluation, ethics, and limitations

### Evidence-informed pilot scorecard

| Indicator | Collection method | Good question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Participation | Count distinct visitors contributing | Who observed or reasoned, not just scanned? |
| Curiosity | Count spontaneous, substantive questions | Did participants ask more probing questions? |
| Learning | One post-tour explanation task | Can visitors support the story with evidence? |
| Delayed memory | 2–3 questions after several days | What details and causal links endured? |
| Group interaction | Observe substantive exchanges | Did visitors build on each other's evidence? |
| Attention to place | Observe where visitors look | Did screens displace observation? |
| Guide workload | Record troubleshooting and delays | Did the activity remain operationally comfortable? |
| Accessibility | Offer equivalent routes/formats | Could everyone participate with dignity? |

**Suggested post-tour questions**
1. What did you notice that you probably would have missed on a conventional tour?
2. At which point were you most curious to learn what happened next?
3. Which clue or location most changed your interpretation?
4. Did any screen interaction interrupt conversation or attention to the place?
5. Would you prefer more, less, or the same amount of digital interaction?

**Safeguards**
- Do not collect location histories, recordings, identifiable photographs, or other personal data without a justified purpose and appropriate consent.
- Be especially cautious with minors and sensitive historical content.
- Mark AI characters, composite testimony, reconstructions, and uncertain interpretations clearly.
- Prefer primary sources, archival images, and verified quotations to fabricated dialogue.
- Ensure each intervention has an oral/print alternative; never assume every visitor has a phone.
- Keep interaction stationary where navigation, roads, stairs, cliffs, or heat could pose risks.
- Do not call satisfaction or novelty a learning outcome. Test comprehension and delayed recall where feasible.

**Research limitations:** The strongest direct study [R1] is single-site. An AR conversational study [R2] reports engagement and exploratory behavior, not superior task accuracy. Human-led narrative study [R3] is a distinct cultural context. Museum and immersive-theater examples demonstrate possibilities but are not direct controlled tests of an independent guide's outdoor tour.

## 16. A tour that changes how people see the world

Imagine meeting a group at the entrance to an old neighborhood. Instead of beginning with dates, the guide holds up a historical photograph:

> “Something important is missing from this street. During our walk, I want you to work out what disappeared, why it vanished, and what its absence tells us about the people who lived here.”

At the first stop, visitors notice an unusual building feature. At the second, an archival photograph contradicts their theory. At the third, two sources disagree. At the fourth, they combine the evidence and discover how the place changed.

There are a few short digital clues, but most of the time visitors look outward, speak to each other, and learn from the guide.

### Best first experiment: **The Mystery of the Vanished Place**

- **Format:** Four stops embedded in an existing tour; approximately 20–30 total minutes of distributed interactions.
- **Materials:** Three research-backed clue cards, one archival image, one optional phone poll, one closing reveal.
- **Development:** A simple static web page or Twine HTML story plus QR codes; printed backup cards.
- **Evaluation:** Visitor observations, quality of discussion, historical understanding, several-day recall, and guide workload.

**The desired outcome is not:** “The technology was impressive.”  
**The desired outcome is:** “I've walked past this place before. I never realized there was so much to discover.”

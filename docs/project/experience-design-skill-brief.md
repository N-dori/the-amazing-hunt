## Addition from the owner: external inspiration and creative research (29 Oct 2026, verbatim)

The owner's introduction: "here is a few ideas for insparation outside of project knowledge".

> ## Additional requirement: External Inspiration & Creative Research
>
> The Educational Experience Design skill must not depend exclusively on existing project knowledge, internal documentation, or a predefined taxonomy of game mechanics.
>
> An essential part of the skill is the ability to **actively discover, investigate, and creatively translate exceptional experiences from the outside world**.
>
> ### 1. Build an external inspiration library
>
> Include a curated reference to these research sources:
>
> **Participatory experiences and immersive storytelling**
> - Coney Archive — https://coneyhq.org/project-archive/
> - Blooloop Immersive Experience Awards — https://blooloop.com/innovation-awards/2025/category/immersive-experience/
>
> **Educational experience design**
> - NEMO Vision of Learning — https://www.nemosciencemuseum.nl/en/research/nemo-vision-of-learning
> - MuseumNext — https://www.museumnext.com/
>
> **Location-based interactive entertainment**
> - Play Disney Parks — https://disneyland.disney.go.com/en-ca/guest-services/play-app/
>
> **Exhibition and spatial experience design**
> - Gensler Digital Experience Design — https://www.gensler.com/expertise/digital-experience-design
>
> These are starting points, not an exhaustive or permanent list.
>
> The skill should be free to discover new sources, creators, studios, research, games, exhibitions, installations, and projects.
>
> ### 2. Search across unrelated creative disciplines
>
> When researching inspiration, deliberately explore beyond educational technology.
>
> Consider:
> - Immersive theater and participatory performance
> - Theme park and attraction design
> - Escape rooms and alternate reality games
> - Interactive documentaries and journalism
> - Museum and science-center exhibitions
> - Location-based adventures and urban games
> - Cooperative board games and party games
> - Experiential storytelling and narrative design
> - Interactive art installations
> - Playful learning and behavioral design
> - Cultural heritage interpretation
> - Children's museums and family experiences
>
> Some of the best educational ideas may originate in industries that have nothing to do with education.
>
> ### 3. Research real experiences, not just abstract techniques
>
> When asked to develop a substantial educational treatment, use web research when available to discover relevant real-world examples.
>
> For each promising precedent, investigate:
>
> 1. What did participants actually do?
> 2. What made the experience compelling?
> 3. Where did curiosity, surprise, emotional investment, or social interaction emerge?
> 4. What role did the physical environment play?
> 5. What was the relationship between the participant and technology?
> 6. What underlying design principle made it work?
> 7. What limitations or failures can be identified?
> 8. How could that principle translate into a simpler, original educational experience?
>
> Use primary sources and detailed case studies where possible.
>
> Distinguish verified details from interpretations and assumptions. Include links to the examples.
>
> ### 4. Extract creative principles — don't copy mechanics
>
> For example:
>
> Don't conclude:
> "Disney uses interactive bounty hunts, so we should add a bounty hunt."
>
> Instead ask:
> "Disney makes visitors feel that an ordinary-looking environment contains a hidden layer of meaning. Can we create that sensation of secret discovery using authentic historical details in a real street?"
>
> Don't conclude:
> "A museum lets visitors interrogate objects, so we should make every historical object talk."
>
> Instead ask:
> "Giving objects distinctive perspectives turns passive information into an encounter. What other techniques could make historical knowledge feel personal and alive?"
>
> The objective is **creative translation**, not imitation.
>
> ### 5. Practice deliberate cross-pollination
>
> For major creative assignments:
>
> - Investigate several meaningfully different external examples.
> - Identify the experience-design principle behind each.
> - Explore how two or more principles could be combined.
> - Propose original treatments grounded in the actual educational material.
> - Evaluate whether the resulting interaction is more meaningful than simply telling the story.
>
> Avoid automatically choosing the most technically impressive precedent.
>
> ### 6. Maintain a living inspiration resource
>
> Create a lightweight external-inspiration reference within the skill's supporting files.
>
> Record especially useful precedents with:
> - Project name and source URL
> - Creative discipline
> - What participants actually experience
> - Underlying design principle
> - Potential educational applications
> - Practical limitations
> - Date checked
>
> Keep this separate from verified historical source material.
>
> External references should inspire HOW we present knowledge, not determine WHAT historical facts we teach.
>
> ### 7. Make the research behavior adaptive
>
> For a small copy improvement, external research may be unnecessary.
>
> For a new mission, major redesign, new interaction format, or overarching experience concept, external research should normally be part of the creative process when web access is available.
>
> When browsing is unavailable, use existing references and explicitly state that no fresh research was performed.
>
> Do not claim to have inspected websites or case studies that were not actually accessed.
>
> ### Core philosophy
>
> **Our project knowledge tells us what stories we have to tell.**
>
> **External creative research expands our imagination of how those stories can be experienced.**
>
> **Educational experience design connects the two.**
>
> The skill should cultivate this ability continuously, without becoming dependent on any specific source, technique, or trend.

# # Run 04 — Synthesis: Research Packet for a Generative Creative-Treatment Methodology

## Instruction

Synthesize Runs 01–03 into a compact, normalized **research packet**.

Do not design software, databases, agents, or a persona-matching engine.
Do not invent new treatments unless needed to clarify a combination already supported by the research.

The purpose is to create a clean evidence base that another content-design model can use later.

## Input

Provide the important tables and closing sections from Runs 01–03 rather than all full reports.

Recommended minimum:

### From Run 01
- Inventory normalization
- Missing treatment families
- New treatment catalogue
- Treatment atoms
- Combination recipes
- Blind spots

### From Run 02
- Question-atom taxonomy
- Question placement
- Questions inside content forms
- Responsive branching
- High-value question recipes
- What the evidence does not establish

### From Run 03
- Evidence table
- Validated family cards
- Constraint matrix
- Transferability audit
- Strongly supported / useful but indirect / professional practice / speculative

## Core synthesis task

Build a normalized language with these layers:

1. TREATMENT
2. INTERACTION ATOM
3. QUESTION ATOM
4. CONTENT FORM
5. GUIDE MOVE
6. GUIDE STANCE
7. CONSTRAINT
8. RENDERER
9. TOUR-LEVEL / LOGISTICAL DESIGN

Do not collapse the layers.

## Output 1 — Canonical definitions

| Layer | Definition | What belongs here | What does not | Examples |

## Output 2 — Canonical treatment families

| Family | Distinctive visitor experience | Included treatments | Typical atoms | Typical content forms | Source fields |

Do not merge treatments merely because they share atoms.

## Output 3 — Canonical treatment catalogue

| Treatment | Family | What happens | Visitor action | Guide action | Key atoms | Compatible question atoms | Content forms | Main risks | Evidence/practice status | Sources |

## Output 4 — Atom library

| Atom | Function | Visitor action | Compatible atoms | Common misuse | Example treatments |

## Output 5 — Question-atom library

| Question atom | Function | Prompt pattern | Best placement | Possible guide branches | Common misuse | Evidence/practice status |

## Output 6 — Content-form transformation map

Use content forms such as:
- timeline;
- testimony;
- life story;
- object;
- photograph;
- map;
- quotation;
- statistic;
- primary source;
- archival video;
- reconstruction;
- oral tradition;
- landscape;
- absence / vanished feature.

| Content form | Treatments that can transform it | Useful atoms | Useful question atoms | Common weak/default treatment |

The purpose is to avoid “timeline = timeline page” thinking.

## Output 7 — Combination grammar

Create reusable recipes such as:

SEARCH + CLUE + INFER + REVEAL  
NOTICE + PREDICT + SHOW + COMPARE  
SOURCE + CHOOSE + DEFEND + CHALLENGE  
STORY + PAUSE + PREDICT + REVEAL  
ROLE + PERFORM + COMPARE + DISCUSS  
MAP + SEARCH + SCALE + REVEAL

Add important recipes discovered by the research.

| Recipe | Visitor experience | Good content forms | Key constraints | Research basis |

## Output 8 — Confidence labels

Define and apply:

- DIRECT EVIDENCE
- INDIRECT EVIDENCE / TRANSFER
- FRAMEWORK / PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE
- PRACTITIONER EXAMPLE
- CREATIVE SYNTHESIS
- EVIDENCE GAP

Explain how these labels should be used.

## Output 9 — What should NOT become deterministic rules

List:
- unsupported persona-to-treatment mappings;
- unvalidated timing rules;
- unvalidated age stereotypes;
- speculative effectiveness claims;
- context-dependent constraints;
- anything else where professional judgment must remain explicit.

## Final deliverable

End with:

# Research-ready foundation

In 500–800 words summarize:
- what a treatment is;
- how treatments differ from atoms and renderers;
- why embedded questions deserve first-class status;
- how content forms can be transformed in multiple ways;
- how constraints and provenance remain stable;
- where evidence is strong;
- where professional judgment and creativity remain necessary.

This is a research synthesis, not a software specification.

## From Run 02 — Question-atom taxonomy


Evidence/practice labels: **L** = live heritage guiding or performance research; **M** = museum visitor research; **H** = historical-thinking or source-study practice; **C** = classroom research or teaching framework; **D** = digital/video study; **P** = professional guidance; **S** = design synthesis. A label identifies the *origin* of support, not proof that the atom works equally well in every setting.

| Question atom | Cognitive/interpretive purpose | Visitor action | Typical prompt form | Best moment | What may be withheld until response | Guide follow-up | Common failure mode | Evidence/practice type | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **NOTICE** | Establish shared, observable evidence. | Point to, describe, hear, or locate a detail. | “What do you notice at the edge of this photograph?” | First encounter with an object, place, image, sound, or video frame. | Caption or explanation. | Point to the named detail; invite one more observation before interpreting. | Asking for an interpretation before anyone has looked. | M; C; P | [R02:web:16][R02:web:54][R02:web:122] |
| **PREDICT** | Make an expectation explicit so subsequent evidence can test it. | State an anticipated event or outcome. | “Given this opening, what do you expect the next account to describe?” | Just before the next story beat, demonstration, or source. | Outcome—not information needed to make a fair prediction. | Show the outcome; ask what confirmed or upset the expectation. | Treating an unknowable surprise as a knowledge test. | M; S | [R02:web:91][R02:web:98] |
| **INFER** | Move from detail to a bounded explanation. | Propose a conclusion and identify its basis. | “What might these wear marks suggest about use?” | After observation, before the guide supplies an interpretation. | One plausible interpretation or corroborating evidence. | Ask which mark supports it; distinguish inference from established fact. | Presenting a plausible guess as proven history. | H; C; P | [R02:web:16][R02:web:54][R02:web:47] |
| **COMPARE** | Detect change, continuity, or meaningful difference. | Identify a specific similarity or contrast. | “What remains in the same position on these two maps?” | After both items are visible or recalled. | The guide’s summary of the difference. | Ask why the difference matters—and whether the sources are comparable. | Comparing items at incompatible scales or dates without warning. | H; M | [R02:web:47][R02:web:54][R02:web:122] |

| **SOURCE CHECK** | Establish what a source can credibly tell us. | Identify creator, date, purpose, audience, or firsthand access. | “Who made this account, and what could they have witnessed?” | Before relying on testimony, a quotation, statistic, or document. | Authorship or production context, if discoverable from the item. | Add context; separate firsthand report from reported belief or hearsay. | Equating a source’s perspective with worthlessness. | H; P | [R02:web:47][R02:web:130][R02:web:122] |

| **BRIDGE** | Give the next stop a question to answer or complicate. | Carry forward a specific puzzle. | “Which question should we take to the next building?” | At the close of a stop or during a transition. | The next stop’s relevant evidence. | Explicitly return to the question on arrival. | Asking for questions and never revisiting them. | M; P; S | [R02:web:98][R02:web:61] |

| **CORROBORATE** | Judge where independent accounts converge or diverge. | Compare claims across sources. | “Which detail appears in both accounts, and which appears in only one?” | Once two sources are available. | A third source or the guide’s assessment. | Check whether the accounts are genuinely independent. | Treating repetition as independent confirmation. | H | [R02:web:47][R02:web:54] |

| **TEST** | Turn an answerable puzzle into a small investigation. | Specify what to inspect, compare, or try. | “What could we check here to distinguish those explanations?” | When accessible evidence could resolve part of a question. | The result of the check. | Carry out the check; interpret what it does and does not show. | Asking a question the available object or site cannot answer. | M; S | [R02:web:98][R02:web:99] |
| **READ THE REPRESENTATION** | Distinguish a depiction from the historical thing depicted. | Identify a map key, image crop, reconstruction choice, scale, or category. | “What has this reconstruction had to fill in?” | Before treating a visualization as evidence. | Production notes or alternative reconstruction. | Label documented elements and interpretive additions. | Presenting a reconstruction or infographic as a transparent window onto the past. | H; P; S | [R02:web:47][R02:web:54] |
| **QUANTIFY / SCALE** | Give a number a meaningful denominator or comparison. | Estimate, calculate, or compare magnitudes. | “Is that total per day or across the whole year—and how would that change your reading?” | When a statistic or spatial scale enters the story. | Denominator, unit, or comparison figure. | Supply units and provenance; revisit the initial impression. | Using a dramatic number without units, source, or context. | H; S | [R02:web:47][R02:web:54] |
| **PERSPECTIVE CHECK** | Notice whose experience a claim describes and whose it omits. | Identify a bounded viewpoint or missing voice. | “Whose view of the street does this diary give us?” | With testimony, life stories, quotations, and institutional records. | A contrasting account, if one exists. | Preserve the speaker’s perspective; introduce other evidence without making the speaker stand for everyone. | Asking visitors to impersonate a historical person or speak for a group. | H; P; L | [R02:web:122][R02:web:61][R02:web:152] |
| **GENERATE** | Produce a visitor-owned, investigable question. | Formulate what they want to find out and how. | “What question could this object actually help us investigate?” | After some unhurried observation. | The guide’s planned question. | Select or adapt a question that the available evidence can address. | Soliciting curiosity when there is no time or means to pursue any of it. | M; C | [R02:web:98][R02:web:54] |

**Withholding is conditional.** Delay an answer only when visitors have enough accessible evidence to do worthwhile work; do not conceal essential context, accessibility information, distressing content warnings, or the source status of an image. Project Zero’s observation routine, for example, separates what is seen from what is inferred, while Library of Congress guidance allows movement back and forth among observing, reflecting, and questioning rather than mandating a rigid sequence. [R02:web:16][R02:web:54]

## From Run 02 — Questions inside content forms


These are **fit-for-purpose combinations**, not claims that each combination has been experimentally tested. “What not to do” identifies a failure specific to the form. Source-study combinations draw particularly on the Library of Congress and historical-reading frameworks; observation combinations draw on museum and visible-thinking practice. | Content form | Question atoms that fit | Example question | Why it works | What not to do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Story / narrative** | PREDICT → COMMIT → REVISE; CHALLENGE | “What does the letter lead you to expect next—and does the next account support it?” | Makes a story beat an evidence check. | Interrupt every beat, or imply that a surprising outcome was predictable. |
| **Timeline** | SEQUENCE → COMPARE → INFER | “Which change came first, and does that establish that it caused the second?” | Separates order from explanation. | Treat adjacent dates as proof of causation. |
| **Map** | NOTICE → READ THE REPRESENTATION → COMPARE | “Which path appears on the older map but not the new one, and what does the key tell us?” | Pairs spatial discovery with map literacy. | Ignore differing scales, boundaries, conventions, or purposes. |

| **Reconstruction** | READ THE REPRESENTATION → CHALLENGE → UNCERTAINTY | “Which parts are evidenced, and which would change if a new foundation were found?” | Exposes documented versus proposed elements. | Present an artist’s decision as an excavated fact. |
| **Infographic / visualization** | READ THE REPRESENTATION → QUANTIFY / SCALE → CHOOSE | “What does each symbol count, and would another denominator change which area looks largest?” | Makes the display’s design consequential. | Ask for a pattern before establishing units and categories. |
| **Video / digital media** | PREDICT → COMMIT → REVISE; NOTICE | “Pause here: which detail do you expect the next frame to clarify?” | A pause gives a specific viewing task and a subsequent check. | Pause merely to solicit a click or repeat the narration. [R02:web:42] |
| **Landscape** | NOTICE → COMPARE → BRIDGE | “Which line in the ground might continue toward our next stop?” | Uses the present place to guide later observation. | Present an indistinct feature as definitively historical. |
| **Absence / vanished feature** | NOTICE → SOURCE CHECK → UNCERTAINTY | “What trace, if any, remains here—and what record tells us what once stood here?” | Treats absence as a question requiring evidence, not as evidence by itself. | Claim that an empty space proves a particular past use. |
| **Drive narration** | PREDICT → NOTICE → BRIDGE; RETRIEVE | “After the bend, look for a change in the roofline; what earlier map might help explain it?” | Gives a brief, forward-facing task that can be completed without conversation. | Demand searching, reading, voting, or prolonged discussion when occupants need to attend to travel and safety. |


## From Run 02 — Responsive branching


Branch on the **answer’s evidential status and the group’s expressed interest**, not on assumed visitor traits. The guide can also decide that the prepared next step remains appropriate—but should make clear how the response informed that decision. Responsive museum facilitation includes observing, offering a fitting challenge or explanation, and sometimes hanging back; facilitation can also displace conversation among family members if the guide becomes its center. [R02:web:91][R02:web:84]

| Visitor response | Appropriate guide move | What not to infer | Example continuation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Correct, with supporting evidence** | Confirm the evidence; advance to a harder implication or contrasting source. | That everyone agrees or needs more difficulty. | “Yes—the dated entry supports that. Does the photograph show the same arrangement?” |
| **Correct, without a stated reason** | Invite a light-touch justification before moving on. | That the answer was a guess or fully understood. | “That fits. Which clue helped you decide?” |
| **Plausible but unsupported** | Revoice as a hypothesis; ask what could test it. | That a plausible answer is a fact—or that its speaker is uninformed. | “That could explain the marks. What would we expect to see elsewhere if it were true?” |
| **Misconception or inaccurate claim** | Identify the particular claim; provide a clear, relevant correction and evidence; offer a chance to revise. | A motive, identity, or fixed belief from one statement. | “The dated plan places the extension later. With that date, how would you adjust the sequence?” |
| **Disagreement within the group** | Ask each side for its strongest evidence; compare sources or state what remains open. | That disagreement needs a vote, or that both positions have equal evidential support. | “You are reading different parts of the map. Let’s check its key before deciding what either line means.” |
| **Silence** | Allow thinking time; shift to an accessible detail, a pair exchange, or an invitation to pass; then continue. | Disinterest, lack of knowledge, or a desire to be singled out. | “Take another moment. You can just point to one detail—or I’ll point out the one I’m curious about.” |
| **Unexpectedly sophisticated answer** | Acknowledge the distinction; invite a concise explanation or a harder counterexample, then reconnect the group. | That everyone wants an extended expert discussion. | “That distinction between when it was written and what it describes matters. Which date should we mark first?” |
| **Unexpected interest or visitor-generated question** | Follow it briefly if evidence and time permit; otherwise carry it to a specified stop or give an honest boundary. | That one person’s interest represents the whole group. | “The next room has a record that bears on that. Let’s hold your question and test it there.” |

Waiting, revoicing, inviting observations, and following visitors’ contributions appear in a study of selected strong museum-facilitation interactions; NPS dialogue guidance also offers pair-share and ways to participate without requiring everyone to speak aloud. Neither source makes silence a diagnosis. [R02:web:122][R02:web:61]


## From Run 02 — High-value question recipes


The arrows mark a **visitor task followed by a guide response or new task**. “REVEAL” means disclose relevant evidence and examine it, not stage a trick. “CONNECT” means apply an earlier idea to the present stop. These are synthesis recipes grounded in the observation, inquiry, historical-source, and responsive-facilitation practices above—not individually tested interventions. [R02:web:16][R02:web:98][R02:web:47][R02:web:91]

| Question recipe | Experience it creates | Where useful |
|---|---|---|
| **NOTICE → COMMIT → REVEAL** | A visible clue supports a provisional answer, then a caption or reverse tests it. | Photographs, objects, cropped images. |
| **NOTICE → INFER → JUSTIFY** | Visitors build an interpretation from a detail they can point to. | Objects, landscapes, artwork, photographs. |
| **NOTICE → UNCERTAINTY → REVEAL** | Visitors identify a genuine limit before a new source supplies some context. | Vanished features, ambiguous objects, photographs. |
| **PREDICT → SHOW SOURCE → REVISE** | The next document changes—or strengthens—an expectation for a stated reason. | Stories, testimony, timelines, video pauses. |
| **PREDICT → SHOW OUTCOME → CHALLENGE** | An outcome becomes an occasion to test the original explanation. | Guided discovery, demonstrations, narrative turns. |
| **COMPARE → INFER → SOURCE CHECK** | An apparent change becomes a historical claim whose sources must be examined. | Maps, paired photographs, before-and-after views. |
| **COMPARE → CORROBORATE → UNCERTAINTY** | Visitors distinguish shared evidence, conflicting claims, and the unresolved remainder. | Two accounts of an event, source packets, testimony. |
| **SOURCE CHECK → QUOTE → CONTEXTUALIZE** | A striking line acquires an author, audience, and historical setting. | Dramatic readings, quotations, letters. |
| **SOURCE CHECK → PERSPECTIVE CHECK → CORROBORATE** | Visitors respect a witness’s account without making it the whole story. | Oral history, life stories, institutional records. |
| **CHOOSE → JUSTIFY → CHALLENGE** | A genuine selection survives or fails a pertinent counterexample. | Competing explanations, which-source-first moments. |
| **COMMIT → DISCUSS → REVISE** | Different initial readings become an evidence-based group exchange. | Maps, reconstructions, short source comparisons. |
| **SEQUENCE → ANCHOR DATE → REVISE** | A timeline is repaired with a documented constraint rather than a correction-by-authority. | Construction phases, migrations, policy changes. |
| **RETRIEVE → CONNECT → BRIDGE** | An earlier clue explains part of this stop and raises a precise question for the next. | Multi-stop walks, drive narration. |
| **GENERATE → SELECT TESTABLE QUESTION → INVESTIGATE** | Visitor curiosity determines a small inquiry the available evidence can sustain. | Interactive objects, accessible archives, paired displays. |
| **NOTICE → TEST → INTERPRET RESULT** | Observation leads to a check and a bounded conclusion. | Material details, visible building phases, manipulable exhibits. |
| **READ THE REPRESENTATION → COMPARE ALTERNATIVE → REVISE** | Visitors recognize that a display makes choices, then test their effect. | Reconstructions, maps, infographics. |
| **QUANTIFY → SUPPLY DENOMINATOR → REASSESS** | A dramatic figure becomes a properly scaled historical claim. | Statistics, migration or production charts. |
| **BRIDGE → FIND TRACE → NAME LIMIT** | A promised search at the next stop pays off—even if the trace is absent. | Landscapes, vanished buildings, transitions. |


## From Run 02 — What the evidence does not establish


It does not establish an optimal number of questions per stop, a universally superior atom or recipe, or that every question should delay an explanation. Nor do classroom retrieval effects, digital question-attempt rates, and science-museum inquiry results automatically establish memory or learning gains on live heritage tours. A question’s success must be judged by what visitors actually **notice, reason about, revise, or investigate—and what the guide does with it**. [R02:web:31][R02:web:42][R02:web:98][R02:web:84]

## From Run 03 — Validated family cards


Each card names *possible work*, not a guaranteed effect. “Digital-enhanced” describes an optional way to present evidence, not a software proposal.

### Evidence investigation
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Assemble a qualified claim from traces; inspect provenance, corroborate, identify a missing record or test competing explanations.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Inspect → source → compare → warrant → qualify.
- **Question atoms that fit:** SOURCE CHECK, NOTICE, INFER, JUSTIFY, CORROBORATE, CHALLENGE, UNCERTAINTY; especially **COMPARE → CORROBORATE → UNCERTAINTY**.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Practising source-based claims and evaluation has classroom-intervention support. **Strength/directness:** empirical **but indirect** for a short tour. **Best-supported setting:** scaffolded secondary-history inquiry.[R03:web:9]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Superficial “eyewitness = reliable” or “photo = proof” judgements; a packet too long for the stop; fabricated certainty. Do not invite a vote on whether documented persecution or deaths happened.
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Attribute testimony; distinguish uncertainty about a *specific detail* from manufactured doubt about well-established harm.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Two attributed reproductions and the visible site trace / an optional zoomable scan with provenance.
- **What is not established:** That a brief guided dossier independently improves historical-thinking skills, or that every apparent corroboration comes from independent sources.[R03:web:9]

### Object and process inquiry
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Examine components and marks; predict a use; inspect or try an authorised replica; correct an inference.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Observe or safely handle → describe → predict → check against documentation.
- **Question atoms that fit:** NOTICE, INFER, TEST, JUSTIFY, UNCERTAINTY; **NOTICE → TEST → INTERPRET RESULT**.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Object recognition and recall; material properties can become discussable. **Strength/directness:** controlled museum evidence for handling-related recall, **indirect** for guided heritage explanation. **Best-supported setting:** touch-permitted museum objects or replicas.[R03:web:13]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Damage, hygiene and handling restrictions; mistaking a replica for an original; sensory distraction; stronger emotional response to disturbing objects. Do not handle fragile originals or culturally restricted material.[R03:web:13][R03:web:91]
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Secure relevant community permission; do not treat human remains or sacred objects as mystery props.[R03:web:91]
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Authorised replica or clear close-up / rotatable, accurately labelled image alongside the actual fabric.
- **What is not established:** That touch alone improves broad factual understanding, procedural competence or historical empathy.[R03:web:13]

### Playful search and puzzle
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Search a bounded safe area, prove a find with a visible detail, combine clues, and revise a proposed pattern.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Locate → verify → exchange → explain; evidence, rather than an arbitrary code, resolves the puzzle.
- **Question atoms that fit:** NOTICE, TEST, COMPARE, CHALLENGE, BRIDGE; **BRIDGE → FIND TRACE → NAME LIMIT**.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Search can organise movement and sustained looking; museum participants reported navigational and learning value. **Strength/directness:** museum field evaluation, **indirect** for learning on guided walks. **Best-supported setting:** ordinary, safely traversable sites with observable clues.[R03:web:28]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Racing, unsafe movement, crowding, device barriers, answer-hunting without interpretation. Avoid game stakes or “escape” premises that trivialise atrocity.[R03:web:28][R03:web:46]
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** At memorials, replace scoring and speed with a quiet, optional evidence search only if site stewards consider it fitting.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Spoken clues or small paper card / optional accessible image of a detail to locate, without mandatory photography.
- **What is not established:** Objective historical learning from the Field Museum hunt, or that escape-game findings transfer to commemorative places.[R03:web:28][R03:web:25]

### Dialogic deliberation
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Examine a shared anchor, listen across perspectives, weigh defensible options and their costs, then identify a reason for retaining or reconsidering a view.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Observe → listen → question assumptions → weigh trade-offs → synthesise; speaking is optional.
- **Question atoms that fit:** SOURCE CHECK, PERSPECTIVE CHECK, CHOOSE, JUSTIFY, CHALLENGE, REVISE.
- **Educational purposes with support:** A structured way to examine civic meanings and competing stewardship choices. **Strength/directness:** direct facilitation **framework**; reported site-evaluation outcomes cannot be assigned specifically to dialogue. **Best-supported setting:** trained facilitation at civic or conscience sites.[R03:web:149][R03:web:23]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Dominant speakers, false equivalence, pressured disclosure and treating a factual atrocity as a debatable proposition. Do not convene an ostensibly “neutral” forum where participants’ safety or dignity cannot be protected.
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Decide which questions are genuinely open—for example, a present-day conservation choice—without making victims’ humanity or documented events negotiable.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Short oral dialogue arc with sourced quotations / accessible attributed quotations shown on a shared display.
- **What is not established:** That a single guide-led forum causes lasting attitude change, reconciliation or civic action.[R03:web:23]

### Peer knowledge exchange
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Each group checks a complementary trace, teaches its finding to others and combines pieces into an answer none held alone.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Investigate → explain → ask for clarification → integrate → guide verifies.
- **Question atoms that fit:** GENERATE, SOURCE CHECK, JUSTIFY, CORROBORATE, RETRIEVE.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Peer explanation and evidence integration are plausible; classroom jigsaw achievement findings are mixed across studies. **Strength/directness:** review of **indirect** classroom evidence. **Best-supported setting:** a small group with time to understand and check each piece.[R03:web:31]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Unequal speaking, inaccurate peer teaching, status competition and poor fit for groups unable to separate and reconvene safely. Avoid assigning anyone a viewpoint because of identity or background.[R03:web:31]
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Share sourced information, never assign a visitor to “represent” a harmed community.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Distinct printed source cards / accessible reproductions viewed in small groups.
- **What is not established:** An advantage over a guide explaining the same evidence during a short tour; more interaction is not automatically better.[R03:web:31]

### Creative translation
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Produce an evidence sketch, bounded caption, then-and-now photograph or optional short written response; mark what is observed versus interpreted.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Look → select → make → annotate → check.
- **Question atoms that fit:** NOTICE, JUSTIFY, READ THE REPRESENTATION, COMPARE, REVISE.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Directed drawing can aid learning in studied instructional contexts. **Strength/directness:** substantial but **indirect** drawing evidence; other products are not validated by that drawing effect. **Best-supported setting:** an observable object or place and a precise, low-pressure prompt.[R03:web:122]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Art-performance anxiety, time and access barriers, unsourced captions, prohibited photography. Do not demand creative expression about someone else’s suffering.
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Check image rights, consent and the site’s rules; make sharing optional and avoid aestheticising remains or atrocity.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Pencil sketch or short paper caption / optional permitted, annotated rephotograph.
- **What is not established:** That poems, photo prompts or caption labs achieve the meta-analysis’s drawing effects—or that a polished artifact means a historically sound interpretation.[R03:web:122]

### Participatory mapping and spatial layering
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Align a dated view, mark a route or omission, or compare documented and lived meanings of a place.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Locate → align or mark → compare → identify map choices → qualify.
- **Question atoms that fit:** COMPARE, READ THE REPRESENTATION, CONTEXTUALIZE, PERSPECTIVE CHECK, UNCERTAINTY.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Community experiential mapping can surface meanings omitted by conventional distribution maps. **Strength/directness:** heritage **case-study** support, not a tested short-tour learning effect. **Best-supported setting:** place-based community fieldwork.[R03:web:151]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** False precision, incompatible map scales or dates, uneven route access, exposing private or restricted locations.
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Do not solicit or publish sacred locations, private routines or vulnerable people’s routes without the relevant authority and consent; distinguish a visitor’s memory from an archival fact. Museum ethics require community involvement and consent for restricted traditional knowledge.[R03:web:91]
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Rights-cleared paper base map or dated photo / optional correctly dated, labelled overlay.
- **What is not established:** That constructing a visitor map improves spatial-historical knowledge more than comparing well-sourced maps with a guide.[R03:web:151]

### Skill, craft and systems demonstration
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Watch a skilled, bounded non-traumatic process; predict a consequential step; optionally try a safe replica action; explain a dependency or bottleneck.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Predict → observe or safely try → sequence → diagnose → debrief.
- **Question atoms that fit:** PREDICT, SEQUENCE, TEST, JUSTIFY, QUANTIFY / SCALE.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Live heritage interpretation supports encounters with objects and space; professional guidance establishes operational boundaries. **Strength/directness:** **direct but nonexperimental** live-interpretation evidence plus practice guidance, not a craft-trial effect. **Best-supported setting:** staffed living-history demonstrations.[R03:web:71][R03:web:142]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Injury, inaccurate technique, presenting one demonstrator’s reconstruction as universal, or confusing feasible replica work with an authentic past experience. No unsafe tools or untrained hands-on trial; safety takes precedence over authenticity.[R03:web:142]
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Demonstrate non-traumatic technologies only when relevant; a craft activity must not purport to simulate forced labour, captivity or victim experience.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Skilled demonstration with a process diagram / optional short, accurately sourced close-up of an otherwise invisible step.
- **What is not established:** That a prediction pause, replica trial or bottleneck diagnosis independently improves retention on live tours.[R03:web:71]

### Contribution and co-curation
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Submit a bounded transcription, evidence-based draft label, investigable question or optional local-knowledge lead for later review.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Produce → source → mark confidence → submit with consent → review.
- **Question atoms that fit:** GENERATE, SOURCE CHECK, JUSTIFY, UNCERTAINTY, PERSPECTIVE CHECK.
- **Educational purposes with support:** A reviewed workflow can produce accurate cultural-collection output. **Strength/directness:** empirical evidence for **online transcription output**, not educational impact or instant tour co-curation. **Best-supported setting:** institutions able to review and govern submissions.[R03:web:81][R03:web:76]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Unverified identifications, extractive collection of local knowledge, privacy and ownership disputes, implied promises that drafts will become official. Do not request a contribution without a real review and disposition pathway.
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Community authority, consent, acknowledgement and equitable treatment matter especially for sacred or Indigenous knowledge; some material should not be recorded or released.[R03:web:91]
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** One authorised excerpt or draft-label sheet with clear collection rules / optional approved submission of a scan-linked transcription.
- **What is not established:** That micro-contributions improve visitor learning, or that a casual live submission reaches the accuracy of Smithsonian’s reviewed output.[R03:web:81]

### Structured reflection
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Record a provisional account privately; after encountering evidence, retain, qualify or change it and name the reason.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Commit privately → check → revise or retain → identify remaining question.
- **Question atoms that fit:** COMMIT, REVISE, JUSTIFY, UNCERTAINTY; **PREDICT → SHOW SOURCE → REVISE**.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Making a change in thinking articulable is the purpose of the Project Zero routine. **Strength/directness:** **framework**, with the proposed tour version a synthesis. **Best-supported setting:** a bounded inquiry with an evidentiary return.[R03:web:139]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Treating change as the “right” outcome, public confession, leading before/after prompts and measuring expressed emotion as learning. Avoid forced disclosure of beliefs or trauma.
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Invite an evidence-based change in *interpretation*, not a declaration of personal moral transformation.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Private mental note or paper slip / optional private, non-shared response.
- **What is not established:** Improved retention, accurate belief revision or durable conduct change from a brief tour close.[R03:web:139]

### Situated mixed-media inquiry
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** At the relevant location, compare an attributed recording, dated image or labelled overlay with what can—and cannot—be observed now.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Source-check → listen or align → inspect place → corroborate → name limits.
- **Question atoms that fit:** SOURCE CHECK, COMPARE, READ THE REPRESENTATION, PERSPECTIVE CHECK, UNCERTAINTY.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Heritage-media literature supports the *possibility* of contextualised interpretation; a same-route tour study warns that managing audio can increase cognitive demand. **Strength/directness:** review plus **direct but nonrandom** tour comparison, neither a test of guide-led located inquiry. **Best-supported setting:** short, optional media comparisons anchored to a visible place.[R03:web:47][R03:web:10]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Device distraction, inaccessible sound, inaccurate registration, reconstruction presented as fact, recording rights and unsafe headphone use while moving.
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Obtain testimony rights; attribute speaker and date; offer an equally substantive non-audio route through the evidence; never use an invented voice as unattributed testimony.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Guide-held dated print or brief authorised reading / optional short recording or labelled overlay examined while stationary.
- **What is not established:** That adding AR or audio to live guiding improves objective learning, or that present fabric can “verify” every aspect of testimony.[R03:web:47][R03:web:10]

### Useful but indirect

Evidence investigation, object inquiry, bounded search, peer exchange, evidence sketching, mapping and situated media all have credible educational or interpretive work to do on a walk. Their expected outcomes should be stated narrowly—such as a better-qualified claim, a checked observation or a disclosed map omission—rather than promised as general learning or attitude gains.[R03:web:9][R03:web:13][R03:web:28][R03:web:31][R03:web:122][R03:web:151][R03:web:47]

### Professional practice

Facilitated dialogue arcs, safe and accurately delimited craft demonstrations, private reflection routines, and consent-and-review pathways are usable professional structures. # Research-ready foundation

A **treatment** is the recognizable sequence through which visitors encounter evidence and do something consequential with it. In an evidence dossier, they form a qualified historical claim; in a clue exchange, they need another visitor’s description to locate a detail; in a dialogue arc, they move from a shared anchor through listening and examination of a genuinely open question. These are different experiences even when all contain observation or discussion. The normalized catalogue retains the supplied inventory’s distinct narrative, sensory, ordering, debate, source-study, and remembrance structures alongside the 52 Run 01 additions. Its examples describe possibilities, not verified site histories or promises of impact.

**Interaction atoms** are smaller actions—SEARCH, SORT, TEACH, ALIGN, TEST. **Question atoms** specify the reasoning sought—SOURCE CHECK, INFER, JUSTIFY, CHALLENGE, UNCERTAINTY. A guide can ask a question inside a story, demonstration, photograph comparison, or transition without thereby inventing a new treatment. **Guide moves** are different again: a pause, correction, revoicing, or disclosure of verified context changes what happens next. **Renderers** merely carry the encounter. Putting chronology cards on a phone leaves chronology reconstruction intact; aligning an old photograph can work with an accurately positioned print rather than augmented reality. An observational routine supports careful seeing and warranted interpretation, but does not dictate one universal sequence of prompts. [web:31][web:41]

Embedded questions deserve first-class status because **placement and follow-up alter their function**. “What do you notice?” can establish common evidence before an explanation. “What would change your mind?” can test a claim after it is voiced. “What can this photograph not show?” can prevent an attractive inference from hardening into fact. Asking without responding is not the same as facilitating inquiry: a supported answer may lead to another source; a plausible but unsupported one to a test; an inaccurate claim to a clear correction; silence to time, another access route, or permission to pass. Withholding an outcome can make a fair prediction meaningful, but cannot justify concealing essential context or a content warning. The packet offers such branches as professional choices, not diagnoses of visitors.

**Content forms do not prescribe treatments.** A timeline might be reconstructed, challenged with a dated anchor, or used to test whether sequence establishes causation. A testimony might sit within parallel voices, a provenance inquiry, or an authorized located comparison. An object might support wear diagnosis, evidence sketching, or alternative captions. Even absence can initiate a bounded search for a corroborating record rather than a claim that an empty space proves a particular history. This multiplicity is what the content-form map preserves: the educational target and permissible evidence should determine the visitor task, not the fact that a source happens to be a map, clip, statistic, or biography.

**Constraints and provenance remain stable when forms and renderers change.** A quotation remains attributable; a replica remains labelled; a reconstruction distinguishes documentation from completion; a source-community restriction does not disappear when material becomes digital. Consent, access, conservation, rights, genuine alternatives to distressing material, and a real review path for contributions are not optional engagement flourishes. At sensitive sites, visitors must not be asked to impersonate victims or debate documented harm. At living sacred places, authority over restricted material and practice must be respected. Site-specific judgment determines whether an otherwise legitimate search, sketch, forum, or ceremony fits. [web:16][web:25]

Evidence is strongest **for particular components in their original settings**, not for these complete, short live-guide treatments. Extended historical inquiry supports source-based work; permitted touching has improved object recall without reliably improving broader factual knowledge; directed drawing has supportive aggregate learning evidence; reviewed transcription can yield accurate output. Jigsaw results are heterogeneous. Live-interpretation research and dialogue frameworks establish relevant practice, while an audio/live tour comparison cautions that added media can increase reported cognitive demand. None supplies a universal ranking of treatments or validates the packet’s newly combined recipes. [web:2][web:67][web:1][web:63][web:46][web:61][web:19][web:48]

The foundation, then, is **generative but not automatic**: preserve the difference between source, task, prompt, facilitation, medium, and ethical boundary; state each proposed outcome narrowly; and label synthesis honestly. Professional judgment remains necessary to select a question worth pursuing, decide what the evidence can settle, adapt to visitor responses, and determine whether the experience is fitting in that place. Creativity remains necessary to make those bounded encounters vivid without converting uncertainty into invention or participation into a claimed benefit that has not been shown.

# DORI Local Stories — Editorial and Research Review

13 October 2026 · First online research pass

## Working principle
Keep the original oral telling intact. Maintain separate (1) original oral transcript, (2) researched factual notes, and (3) concise performance script. Mark childhood memories, family traditions, folklore, and reported history differently. The online findings below provide leads; they do not establish all of the stories' details.

## 001 — Rachmo: The Restaurant That Fed Jerusalem
**Best dramatic arc:** Poor taxi driver / uncertain business → small family restaurant → food and generosity → local affection → enduring institution.

**Important correction:** A Ynet interview with granddaughters Liron Keren and Merav Nehmias identifies founders Rahamim (Rachmo) and **Rivka Ben Yosef**, and dates the restaurant to **1954**. It reports that Rivka worked as a cook in Yekke homes and proposed opening a small food outlet because the family's income was insufficient. Its original dishes were rice and beans, hummus, bean soup, and a meatball. This supports the taxi-driver origin, but conflicts with the oral version's naming of his wife as Rachel. Source: https://www.ynet.co.il/food/article/yokra14527086

**Another source:** A Ynet remembrance of Azura founder Ezra Sharfler says he worked as a teenager in Rachmo's initial kiosk near a taxi stand and later helped it become a restaurant. Source: https://www.ynet.co.il/food/foodnews/article/r1sgi11woo

**Hold as unverified oral details:** Loan-giver dialogue, addiction, the fish-stand poem and poet's exact identity, visitors' rice/beans in pita, the 1967 generals' celebration. The “seven Arab armies” phrase is historically overbroad for the Six-Day War and should not appear as an unqualified fact.

**Editorial treatment:** Open with the cafeteria queue and a visitor who cannot pay. Delay the restaurant's later celebrity until the end. Let the 1954 family story establish setting; frame unverified kindness anecdotes as stories people tell.

**Possible hook:** “What makes a restaurant famous—the people who eat there, or the people it refuses to turn away?”

## 002 — How the market grew, and its afterlives
**Best structure:** Divide into three episodes rather than making every anecdote do the same work: (A) the neighborhood creates a market; (B) the market remembers its colorful characters; (C) closed shutters reveal an art gallery.

**Historical backbone:** Market histories describe informal trade near Beit Yaakov in the late nineteenth century, including Arab agricultural vendors selling to new Jewish neighborhoods outside the walls; the land is described as belonging to the Valero family. Under British administration, market stalls and sanitation were reorganized. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahane_Yehuda_Market and https://oritkropptours.com/blog/history-of-mahane-yehuda-market

**Solomon Souza:** Israel's mural-art survey documents portraits on the shutters, many rabbis but also artists, scientists, politicians and vendors. The survey dates projects from 2015 onward. Source: https://www.wallart.org.il/artworks/%D7%AA%D7%A8%D7%99%D7%A1%D7%99%D7%9D-%D7%9E%D7%A6%D7%95%D7%99%D7%A8%D7%99%D7%9D/

**Casino de Paris:** A first-person recollection by Levana Molcho describes the Mandate-era building, decorative rose-patterned tiles and courtyard, and an account of casino/hotel uses. Source: https://www.mesaper.co.il/Screens/Story_2886504d-575a-433b-85ba-ce6eedadfaf2.html

**Hold as folklore/unverified:** $100-banknotes standing upright joke; vendor Pinchas's fireworks; the claim that the first ten murals were all rabbis or that public complaints directly changed artistic selection; derivation of “Georgian Market”; currently permitted rooftop access.

**Editorial treatment:** One market, several audiences and times: early-morning vendors, Shabbat art viewers, evening diners. Select only one childhood joke per live stop. Do not repeat instructions for accessing private roofs without confirmation.

## 003 — Raphael and the man searching for luck
**Best arc:** The visitor hears three requests for help; on the return journey each offer becomes a missed opportunity; the lion delivers the punchline.

**Local lead:** The market website lists *Atliz Rafael* at HaHaruv 12 corner of HaTut, saying it was founded by Raphael, father of Moshe Tzikbashvili, and describes a Georgian butchering tradition. This might be the same Raphael but identity is not independently confirmed. Source: https://www.machne.co.il/category/%D7%90%D7%99%D7%98%D7%9C%D7%99%D7%96-%D7%A8%D7%A4%D7%90%D7%9C

**Hold as oral history:** Raphael reading coffee grounds, his interactions with unhappy customers, the folktale as his customary advice, and the date of his death. Avoid attaching these assertions to the directory's Raphael until confirmed.

**Editorial treatment:** Ask visitors to predict whether the traveler will take the gold after the first offer. Build the second refusal as comic repetition. Do not explain the moral before the lion's note. Best closing line: “Was he unlucky—or did he keep walking past his luck?”

## 004 — The blue roof, Lilith and inherited superstitions
**Best structure:** Two linked but distinct micro-stories: (A) sky-blue and protection; (B) the star and the doorstep as family memory. The bridge is about explanations that outlive their original circumstances.

**Verified cultural background:** The National Library of Israel holds an infant-protection amulet from nineteenth-century Ukraine referring to Lilith, Elijah and protective words. Scholar and Jewish folklore sources document the association of Lilith with childbirth dangers and amulets. Sources: https://www.nli.org.il/en/education/teaching-resources/primary-sources/nnl_edu997013134913505171 ; https://encyclopedia.yivo.org/article/2093

**Crucial distinction:** Those sources do **not** verify that Mahane Yehuda's roof was painted light blue for protection from Lilith, nor that blue frames were universally used in nineteenth-century Jerusalem. Treat that as a suggested folk interpretation, not a documented causal history.

**Family history:** Keep the warning against pointing at stars and the treatment of the doorway as personal testimony. Do not present their origin in the Spanish Inquisition as proven; practices of respecting mezuzot and doorposts also exist in Jewish custom independent of crypto-Jewish persecution. Source: https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/312461/jewish/The-Laws-and-Customs.htm

**Historical precision:** The Alhambra Decree was in 1492; the Inquisition in Spain began earlier (1478), not in response to the expulsion alone. Some forced converts continued Jewish observance secretly, but their experiences varied. Avoid implying every expelled Jew became a crypto-Jew.

**Editorial treatment:** Start by asking visitors to look up and notice the roof color. Pose two different questions: “Why would a committee choose this color?” and “Why might a family once have painted a doorway blue?” The answers may be different. For the star story, quote your mother's warning before offering the later interpretation.

## 005 — Nachlaot: Heart of Jerusalem
**Best anchor:** Stand in a real courtyard and notice a cistern, the enclosing walls and reuse of materials.

**Historical grounding:** Nachlaot is an umbrella designation for courtyard neighborhoods built west of the Old City from the 1870s onward. The count of neighborhoods varies across sources: “32” appears frequently but other counts such as 23 also occur. The numerical pun *lev = 32* is sound gematria, but it does not establish one official neighborhood count. Sources: https://earolam.co.il/earzion/nachlaot/ ; https://remaxjerusalem.com/en/neighborhoods/nachlaot

**Correction:** The communal water feature was typically a **rainwater cistern**, not a groundwater well. A local guide specifically distinguishes these because groundwater is far below the surface. Source: https://www.israelprivatetouring.com/jerusalem-neighborhoods.html

**Hold as local interpretation:** The red roofs forming a heart viewed from above, a precise etymology of “abaday,” the Robin Hood-like redistribution story, roofs made from oil barrel metal, and “most expensive real estate in the country.” Avoid a sweeping characterization of Ottoman officials as uniformly corrupt or indifferent.

**Editorial treatment:** Beginning with an object is stronger than beginning with the 32-neighborhood statistic: “What was this courtyard built to keep in—and what was it built to keep out?” Reveal communal water, privacy, mutual aid and protection. End with the reversal: a poor courtyard dwelling can become desirable urban property.

## Common performance advice
1. Every stop should start with one visible object, place, person or unresolved question.
2. Limit each stop to one main narrative arc and one memorable reveal. Make optional side stories available only when time and interest allow.
3. Retain authentic jokes and dialog, but quote historic people only when the words are documented; otherwise say “as the story is told.”
4. Distinguish **verified historical record**, **published recollection**, **local oral tradition**, **personal memory**, and **creative interpretation**.
5. Avoid the phrase “most famous”, “first”, “everyone”, “always”, or “most expensive” unless sourced.
6. Ask a question that visitors can answer from observation or reasoning before giving the explanation.
7. Never sacrifice a great punchline by explaining the moral in advance.
8. Keep long researched notes off the spoken script; make them available as supporting reference.

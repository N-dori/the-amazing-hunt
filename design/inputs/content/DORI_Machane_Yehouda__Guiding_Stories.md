# Rachmo — The Restaurant That Wouldn’t Let You Leave Hungry

**DORI Local Stories | Refined guiding script | October 2026**

## Spoken story

Come, let me show you something. You see this restaurant? It’s called Rachmo. Nothing fancy. No white tablecloths. You take a tray, you stand in line, and when you reach the counter you tell the person what you want. Very Jerusalem. No ceremony.

But I want to tell you how this little restaurant became part of the city’s memory.

The founder was Rahamim Ben Yosef—everybody called him Rachmo. His parents came from the region of Turkey near Syria. Before he became a restaurateur, Rachmo drove a taxi. The money wasn't enough. His wife, Rivka, worked as a cook, and according to their granddaughters she was the one who suggested they try a little food business in the market. In 1954, they opened a modest place serving simple food to working people: beans, rice, hummus, soup, meatballs. Food that fills you up.

And it was not easy. There is a family story about Rachmo deciding he had had enough and wanted to close. Rivka wasn't having it. In one version she more or less told him, “You can go back to whatever you were doing. I'm keeping the place.” And she did. That's how I like to tell this story: behind the man whose name is on the sign stood a woman who refused to give up.

Now, there's another story people tell here. Not a business record—a market story. Sometimes a customer would reach the counter, look at the food, and make that face. You know the face: “Today I really don't have the money.” Rachmo would see it. And rather than let somebody walk away hungry, he would find something simple to give them. Rice and beans in pita—nothing luxurious, but a meal. To him, a good name was worth more than a rich man's fortune.

And here comes the lovely reversal. According to local lore, after the Six-Day War in 1967, prominent Israeli military officers came looking for somewhere to celebrate. Where did Jerusalemites send them? To Rachmo. The story says photographs and publicity followed. I haven't found firm documentation for that episode, so I tell it as the market remembers it, not as a proved turning point.

Today, the business is associated with the next generations of the family. So when you stand in this line, remember: the restaurant didn't start with celebrity chefs. It started with a taxi driver, a determined cook named Rivka, and food ordinary people could afford.

And here's my question: what really makes a restaurant famous? The famous people who come to eat—or the people it refuses to send away hungry?

# Mahane Yehuda — A Market with More Than One Life

**DORI Local Stories | Refined guiding script | October 2026**

## Spoken story

Before you look at the shops, look at the neighborhood around them. Because here's the thing: Mahane Yehuda didn't create its neighborhood. In a way, the neighborhoods created the market.

At the end of the nineteenth century, Jerusalem was expanding beyond the Old City walls. People needed somewhere nearby to buy food. Arab farmers and vendors brought fruits and vegetables from the surrounding villages and sold them on open ground near the new Jewish neighborhoods. One of the plots belonged to the Valero family. The place grew gradually—first informal stalls, then more permanent trade. People from different communities came here because people needed to eat. That is one of the simplest, and most interesting, beginnings a market can have.

Then came the British. After 1917, they looked at the stalls and started talking about planning, sanitation, proper infrastructure. The market was no longer just a convenient patch of open ground. It was becoming an institution. There's a lovely joke told here about the bankers and the cost of buying the ground: “Cover the place with banknotes,” a rich man supposedly said. The merchants collected enough to lay notes across the ground. “No,” he answered. “I meant standing upright!” Of course it's a joke. But it catches something true about how expensive and difficult building a market could be.

For me, though, the market is not only dates and planning. I remember a vendor named Pinchas. Children knew to come to him around the holidays and ask for fireworks. Illegal? Certainly. Secret? Not really. “Who told you about me?” he'd ask. Everybody knew! That was part of the charm of a place where people know each other a little too well.

Now come here on a Friday evening or Shabbat, when so many shutters are closed, and you'll discover a second market. The doors go down and the portraits come out. The artist Solomon Souza painted hundreds of metal shutters with rabbis, musicians, public figures, historical characters, and even market people themselves. I tell a story about his early commissions: he asked merchants whom they wanted painted, and so many requested rabbis that the market began to look like a rabbinical hall of fame. Later the subjects became much more varied. That sequence is a local telling; what we can see for ourselves is the extraordinary variety now on the shutters.

And over there you'll hear the name Casino de Paris—a place with stories from the British Mandate era, old floor tiles and a later life as a bar. In another corner people speak of the Georgian Market. Some names here tell you about communities; some come with stories no one quite agrees on.

So, one last thought as we walk: in the morning this is a food market. At night, parts of it become a place for dinner and drinks. On Shabbat the metal shutters become an art gallery. Same streets. Three completely different experiences. Which one is the real Mahane Yehuda? I would say: all of them.

# Raphael the Butcher — The Man Who Went Looking for Luck

**DORI Local Stories | Refined guiding script | October 2026**

## Spoken story

Let me tell you about a butcher people remember as Raphael. And according to the story I heard, he had a second occupation. Not the one that paid the bills. Sometimes he would sit with people over a little cup of coffee, turn it over, look at the grounds, and talk about their future.

He noticed something. Very different people came to him, but many said exactly the same thing: “If only I had a little luck.” And so, the story goes, Raphael told them a folktale. Now, it's not a true story. Listen to the point.

Once there was a man who was convinced he had no luck. “That's it,” he said. “I'm going to find my luck, and I'm not coming back until I do.” Off he went.

First he met a lion. “Why are you running?” the lion asked. “I'm looking for my luck!” The lion said, “Fine. When you find it, ask why my teeth are weak and I can't catch my prey anymore.” “I'll ask!” said the man. And he hurried away.

Next he stopped beneath a tree. “Where are you going?” asked the tree. “To find my luck!” “Then ask why I barely grow any fruit these days.” “Of course,” said the man, already getting up.

Then he reached a farmer struggling with a field. “Young man, can you give me a hand?” “Sorry! No time. I'm on an important journey. I'm going to find my destiny.” And off he went again.

Mountains. Rivers. Snow. Forests. Finally, he reached a cave. He entered, found the mysterious being he believed was his luck, and received the answers he wanted. He was thrilled. At last! His luck had been found.

On the way home, he passed the farmer. “Did you get an answer for me?” “Yes! Your soil is fine. There's gold buried underneath, and that's why you're having trouble working it.” “Wonderful,” said the farmer. “Help me dig it up. Keep some gold for yourself!” “Me? No! I'm busy. I have a destiny to get back to.”

Then came the tree. “And my fruit?” “A great diamond is pressing on your roots. Take it out and you'll grow fruit again.” “I'm a tree,” said the tree. “I can't dig. Please, take the diamond!” “No time!” the man replied.

At last he reached the lion. “Did you ask about my teeth?” “Yes,” said the man. “I was given a note for you.” The lion read it, folded it very carefully and put it away.

“What does it say?” asked the man.

“Are you sure you want to know?”

“Of course!”

“It says: **If that idiot comes back, eat him.**”

[Pause. Let people laugh.]

And that's where Raphael stopped. You see, the poor man had passed a whole field of gold and a diamond bigger than his troubles. He was so busy searching for luck that he couldn't recognize it when it stood right in front of him.

So—was he unlucky? Or was he simply not looking?

# The Blue Roof — What We Remember Without Knowing Why

**DORI Local Stories | Refined guiding script | October 2026**

## Spoken story

Before we go any further, look up. What color do you see? Light blue. Why light blue? Well, according to a story told among the merchants, when a new roof covering was being discussed, that was the color everybody wanted. I can't confirm how the vote happened, or why the committee chose it. But let me give you a possible Jerusalem explanation—one that takes us into the world of old customs.

Two hundred years ago, life was much more fragile than it is today. Parents were terrified of losing newborn babies. They didn't always understand why a child became ill or died. And when we humans cannot explain something frightening, we look for a story that gives it shape.

One of those stories was about Lilith, a dangerous figure in Jewish folklore associated with threats to infants and mothers. Families used prayers, names and protective amulets. We have actual surviving examples—this is not something I need to invent. But there's also a local explanation you may hear about houses painted with sky blue near their doors and windows: the color of heaven would frighten away harmful beings. Is that why this market roof is blue? I don't know. It's an attractive story, not proof of the committee's intentions.

And that reminds me of two things from my childhood. My mother used to say, “Don't point at a star. You'll get a blister on your finger.” What? Why? Nobody could really explain it to me. Later, at university, when I studied the history of Jews who secretly maintained their faith in Christian Spain, I wondered: could a warning like that have discouraged children from looking for the three stars that traditionally mark the end of Shabbat? It clicked for me. But I want to be careful: that's my interpretation, not a demonstrated origin of the saying.

You see, in Spain the Inquisition was established in 1478, and the expulsion of Jews followed in 1492. Some Jews converted and some of these converts continued Jewish practices secretly, sometimes under terrible danger. Families concealed customs, and across generations people could remember the rule without remembering the reason. Not every later Jewish superstition comes from the Inquisition. Still, that history made me hear my mother's warning differently.

She had another rule. When she washed the floor, she would never sweep dirty water out across the doorway. No garbage beside the door. No shoes there. To her the threshold deserved respect. As a child I simply obeyed. Only later did I think about the mezuzah, the special meaning of a Jewish doorway, and how an ordinary house can hold invisible boundaries.

So that's the story I'd like you to carry with you. We can verify that people used protective traditions. We can remember what our own parents taught us. And we can explore the explanations—but we don't have to pretend we know exactly where every custom began.

How many things do you still do today because someone once said, “That's just how we do it”?

# Nachlaot — The Heart of the City

**DORI Local Stories | Refined guiding script | October 2026**

## Spoken story

Come into this courtyard with me. And before I tell you anything, just look around. Small houses, facing one another, not the street. A quiet space in the middle. In many of the old Jerusalem courtyards, there was also a cistern for collecting rainwater. So what are we looking at—architecture? Or a little society?

At the end of the nineteenth century Jerusalem was spreading beyond the Old City walls. People were moving out, but leaving the walls behind wasn't just a question of comfort. It could also feel exposed. So many new neighborhoods were built around courtyards, often inhabited by families who shared a place of origin, a language, a tradition, or a synagogue. Here in Nachlaot you could meet Yemenite Jews, Kurdish Jews, Sephardi families, Ashkenazi families—and many others. It's a collection of neighborhoods, not one uniform community.

This courtyard was more than empty space. Children played here. People exchanged news, cooked, worked, argued. Young people met and—who knows?—fell in love. The cistern mattered because water mattered. And the surrounding walls offered a degree of privacy and protection. The structure encouraged people to rely on each other.

And then there's the name. Nachlaot is the familiar name for this patchwork of neighborhoods; you'll also see the wider municipal area called Lev Ha'Ir—the heart of the city. There's a beautiful play on the Hebrew word *lev*, heart. Lamed is 30. Bet is 2. Together: 32. Some storytellers link that to 32 neighborhoods. Lovely, isn't it? We should take the number as a traditional shorthand, because historical counts vary according to what you include. Some people even say the red-tiled roofs look like a heart from above. Maybe one day we'll find the perfect aerial photo to test that.

Now, here's another character who belongs to the neighborhood's folklore: the *abaday*—a tough neighborhood strongman, somebody people were wary of and sometimes depended upon. The story says he could walk through the market and take food from the vendors. No one challenged him. But the tale insists he wasn't gathering it for his own table. He carried it back to people in his community. A kind of Robin Hood—with a slightly uncomfortable detail: the vendors weren't exactly asked for permission. I like that complication. Was he a protector? A bully? Both? The story lets us ask.

Look also at the old buildings. Some have layers of improvised metal roofing. People remember cramped rooms—hot in summer, cold in winter—and repairs made from whatever materials were available. The story about reusing the sheet metal of oil barrels is one I still want to check against the buildings themselves.

Now for the irony. These courtyards were not built as picturesque heritage apartments. Some of the residents struggled terribly. For years parts of Nachlaot were associated with poverty. Today the same intimate streets and old stone houses are sought after by people who can afford expensive central Jerusalem property.

So, what changed? The walls? The stones? Not entirely. What changed was the way the city valued them. What once meant necessity and hardship can later be sold as charm.

And whenever I walk through here, I try to remember the people who didn't have the luxury of calling it charming.

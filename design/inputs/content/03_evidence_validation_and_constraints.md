# # Run 03 — Evidence Validation and Constraints for High-Value Treatment Families

## Instruction

Use the outputs from:
- Run 01 — Creative Treatments Gap Discovery
- Run 02 — Embedded Questions Deep Dive

Select the **8–12 most important treatment families** that appear most promising for live heritage guiding and validate them more deeply.

Do not design software.
Do not assign methods to demographic personas.
Do not rank the families.

The goal is to distinguish:
- direct empirical support;
- reviews/frameworks;
- professional practice;
- plausible transfer from another field;
- speculation.

## Input

Provide:
1. Run 01's “Missing treatment families” table;
2. the most important new-treatment rows from Run 01;
3. Run 02's question-atom taxonomy and high-value recipes.

Do not paste the full reports if unnecessary.

## Selection rule

Prefer families that:
- create genuinely different visitor experiences;
- are reusable across many heritage subjects;
- include several distinct treatments;
- plausibly transfer to live place-based guiding;
- are not already overrepresented in conventional guide training.

## Research questions

For each selected family:
1. What educational/interpretive work can it plausibly perform?
2. What empirical evidence exists?
3. What settings/populations were studied?
4. How transferable is it to live heritage guiding?
5. Which visitor actions make it distinct?
6. What risks/failure modes are documented?
7. What contraindications or sensitive-site constraints apply?
8. Which claims remain professional practice or synthesis rather than evidence?

## From Run 02 — Question-atom taxonomy


Evidence/practice labels: **L** = live heritage guiding or performance research; **M** = museum visitor research; **H** = historical-thinking or source-study practice; **C** = classroom research or teaching framework; **D** = digital/video study; **P** = professional guidance; **S** = design synthesis. A label identifies the *origin* of support, not proof that the atom works equally well in every setting.

| Question atom | Cognitive/interpretive purpose | Visitor action | Typical prompt form | Best moment | What may be withheld until response | Guide follow-up | Common failure mode | Evidence/practice type | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **NOTICE** | Establish shared, observable evidence. | Point to, describe, hear, or locate a detail. | “What do you notice at the edge of this photograph?” | First encounter with an object, place, image, sound, or video frame. | Caption or explanation. | Point to the named detail; invite one more observation before interpreting. | Asking for an interpretation before anyone has looked. | M; C; P | [R02:web:16][R02:web:54][R02:web:122] |
| **PREDICT** | Make an expectation explicit so subsequent evidence can test it. | State an anticipated event or outcome. | “Given this opening, what do you expect the next account to describe?” | Just before the next story beat, demonstration, or source. | Outcome—not information needed to make a fair prediction. | Show the outcome; ask what confirmed or upset the expectation. | Treating an unknowable surprise as a knowledge test. | M; S | [R02:web:91][R02:web:98] |
| **INFER** | Move from detail to a bounded explanation. | Propose a conclusion and identify its basis. | “What might these wear marks suggest about use?” | After observation, before the guide supplies an interpretation. | One plausible interpretation or corroborating evidence. | Ask which mark supports it; distinguish inference from established fact. | Presenting a plausible guess as proven history. | H; C; P | [R02:web:16][R02:web:54][R02:web:47] |
| **COMPARE** | Detect change, continuity, or meaningful difference. | Identify a specific similarity or contrast. | “What remains in the same position on these two maps?” | After both items are visible or recalled. | The guide’s summary of the difference. | Ask why the difference matters—and whether the sources are comparable. | Comparing items at incompatible scales or dates without warning. | H; M | [R02:web:47][R02:web:54][R02:web:122] |
| **CHOOSE** | Prioritize among defensible options. | Select an explanation, route of inquiry, or evidence item. | “Which of these two sources would you examine first to check that claim?” | At a genuine interpretive fork. | The guide’s preferred answer or next evidence item. | Follow the chosen line briefly; explain what the other option could add. | Offering choices that cannot change the discussion. | P; C; S | [R02:web:61][R02:web:80] |
| **SEQUENCE** | Reconstruct temporal or causal order. | Put events, images, or decisions in order. | “Which had to happen before this building could operate?” | After enough events have been introduced. | The completed timeline. | Supply a dated anchor; distinguish documented order from inferred causation. | Making chronology into a speed-memory quiz. | H; S | [R02:web:47][R02:web:54] |
| **RETRIEVE** | Reactivate something encountered earlier for use now. | Recall a clue, claim, or unresolved question. | “What did we see at the gate that helps us read this ledger?” | At a later stop with a meaningful connection. | The previously discussed clue, briefly. | Restate it if needed, then apply it to the present evidence. | Testing an obscure name or date that has no interpretive payoff. | C; S | [R02:web:31][R02:web:43] |

| **SOURCE CHECK** | Establish what a source can credibly tell us. | Identify creator, date, purpose, audience, or firsthand access. | “Who made this account, and what could they have witnessed?” | Before relying on testimony, a quotation, statistic, or document. | Authorship or production context, if discoverable from the item. | Add context; separate firsthand report from reported belief or hearsay. | Equating a source’s perspective with worthlessness. | H; P | [R02:web:47][R02:web:130][R02:web:122] |

| **TEST** | Turn an answerable puzzle into a small investigation. | Specify what to inspect, compare, or try. | “What could we check here to distinguish those explanations?” | When accessible evidence could resolve part of a question. | The result of the check. | Carry out the check; interpret what it does and does not show. | Asking a question the available object or site cannot answer. | M; S | [R02:web:98][R02:web:99] |
| **READ THE REPRESENTATION** | Distinguish a depiction from the historical thing depicted. | Identify a map key, image crop, reconstruction choice, scale, or category. | “What has this reconstruction had to fill in?” | Before treating a visualization as evidence. | Production notes or alternative reconstruction. | Label documented elements and interpretive additions. | Presenting a reconstruction or infographic as a transparent window onto the past. | H; P; S | [R02:web:47][R02:web:54] |
| **QUANTIFY / SCALE** | Give a number a meaningful denominator or comparison. | Estimate, calculate, or compare magnitudes. | “Is that total per day or across the whole year—and how would that change your reading?” | When a statistic or spatial scale enters the story. | Denominator, unit, or comparison figure. | Supply units and provenance; revisit the initial impression. | Using a dramatic number without units, source, or context. | H; S | [R02:web:47][R02:web:54] |
| **PERSPECTIVE CHECK** | Notice whose experience a claim describes and whose it omits. | Identify a bounded viewpoint or missing voice. | “Whose view of the street does this diary give us?” | With testimony, life stories, quotations, and institutional records. | A contrasting account, if one exists. | Preserve the speaker’s perspective; introduce other evidence without making the speaker stand for everyone. | Asking visitors to impersonate a historical person or speak for a group. | H; P; L | [R02:web:122][R02:web:61][R02:web:152] |
| **GENERATE** | Produce a visitor-owned, investigable question. | Formulate what they want to find out and how. | “What question could this object actually help us investigate?” | After some unhurried observation. | The guide’s planned question. | Select or adapt a question that the available evidence can address. | Soliciting curiosity when there is no time or means to pursue any of it. | M; C | [R02:web:98][R02:web:54] |

**Withholding is conditional.** Delay an answer only when visitors have enough accessible evidence to do worthwhile work; do not conceal essential context, accessibility information, distressing content warnings, or the source status of an image. Project Zero’s observation routine, for example, separates what is seen from what is inferred, while Library of Congress guidance allows movement back and forth among observing, reflecting, and questioning rather than mandating a rigid sequence. # Evidence validation for live heritage guiding

**Eleven treatment families meet the selection rule.** They offer different visitor actions—investigating, handling, searching, deliberating, teaching, making, mapping, trying a process, contributing, revising, and checking media against place. None is ranked. The important distinction is between evidence that a *component* works elsewhere and evidence that the *proposed live-guided treatment* works; the latter is scarce.

The selections use Run 01’s missing-family and treatment tables and Run 02’s question atoms and recipes. A recipe such as **NOTICE → INFER → JUSTIFY** is a proposed facilitation sequence, **not** an independently tested intervention. Across families, a guide should disclose source status, essential context, accessibility information, and content warnings before asking visitors to interpret evidence.

## Output 2 — Validated family cards

Each card names *possible work*, not a guaranteed effect. “Digital-enhanced” describes an optional way to present evidence, not a software proposal.

### Evidence investigation
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Assemble a qualified claim from traces; inspect provenance, corroborate, identify a missing record or test competing explanations.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Inspect → source → compare → warrant → qualify.
- **Question atoms that fit:** SOURCE CHECK, NOTICE, INFER, JUSTIFY, CORROBORATE, CHALLENGE, UNCERTAINTY; especially **COMPARE → CORROBORATE → UNCERTAINTY**.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Practising source-based claims and evaluation has classroom-intervention support. **Strength/directness:** empirical **but indirect** for a short tour. **Best-supported setting:** scaffolded secondary-history inquiry.[web:9]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Superficial “eyewitness = reliable” or “photo = proof” judgements; a packet too long for the stop; fabricated certainty. Do not invite a vote on whether documented persecution or deaths happened.
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Attribute testimony; distinguish uncertainty about a *specific detail* from manufactured doubt about well-established harm.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Two attributed reproductions and the visible site trace / an optional zoomable scan with provenance.
- **What is not established:** That a brief guided dossier independently improves historical-thinking skills, or that every apparent corroboration comes from independent sources.[web:9]

### Object and process inquiry
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Examine components and marks; predict a use; inspect or try an authorised replica; correct an inference.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Observe or safely handle → describe → predict → check against documentation.
- **Question atoms that fit:** NOTICE, INFER, TEST, JUSTIFY, UNCERTAINTY; **NOTICE → TEST → INTERPRET RESULT**.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Object recognition and recall; material properties can become discussable. **Strength/directness:** controlled museum evidence for handling-related recall, **indirect** for guided heritage explanation. **Best-supported setting:** touch-permitted museum objects or replicas.[web:13]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Damage, hygiene and handling restrictions; mistaking a replica for an original; sensory distraction; stronger emotional response to disturbing objects. Do not handle fragile originals or culturally restricted material.[web:13][web:91]
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Secure relevant community permission; do not treat human remains or sacred objects as mystery props.[web:91]
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Authorised replica or clear close-up / rotatable, accurately labelled image alongside the actual fabric.
- **What is not established:** That touch alone improves broad factual understanding, procedural competence or historical empathy.[web:13]

### Playful search and puzzle
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Search a bounded safe area, prove a find with a visible detail, combine clues, and revise a proposed pattern.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Locate → verify → exchange → explain; evidence, rather than an arbitrary code, resolves the puzzle.
- **Question atoms that fit:** NOTICE, TEST, COMPARE, CHALLENGE, BRIDGE; **BRIDGE → FIND TRACE → NAME LIMIT**.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Search can organise movement and sustained looking; museum participants reported navigational and learning value. **Strength/directness:** museum field evaluation, **indirect** for learning on guided walks. **Best-supported setting:** ordinary, safely traversable sites with observable clues.[web:28]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Racing, unsafe movement, crowding, device barriers, answer-hunting without interpretation. Avoid game stakes or “escape” premises that trivialise atrocity.[web:28][web:46]
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** At memorials, replace scoring and speed with a quiet, optional evidence search only if site stewards consider it fitting.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Spoken clues or small paper card / optional accessible image of a detail to locate, without mandatory photography.
- **What is not established:** Objective historical learning from the Field Museum hunt, or that escape-game findings transfer to commemorative places.[web:28][web:25]

### Dialogic deliberation
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Examine a shared anchor, listen across perspectives, weigh defensible options and their costs, then identify a reason for retaining or reconsidering a view.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Observe → listen → question assumptions → weigh trade-offs → synthesise; speaking is optional.
- **Question atoms that fit:** SOURCE CHECK, PERSPECTIVE CHECK, CHOOSE, JUSTIFY, CHALLENGE, REVISE.
- **Educational purposes with support:** A structured way to examine civic meanings and competing stewardship choices. **Strength/directness:** direct facilitation **framework**; reported site-evaluation outcomes cannot be assigned specifically to dialogue. **Best-supported setting:** trained facilitation at civic or conscience sites.[web:149][web:23]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Dominant speakers, false equivalence, pressured disclosure and treating a factual atrocity as a debatable proposition. Do not convene an ostensibly “neutral” forum where participants’ safety or dignity cannot be protected.
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Decide which questions are genuinely open—for example, a present-day conservation choice—without making victims’ humanity or documented events negotiable.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Short oral dialogue arc with sourced quotations / accessible attributed quotations shown on a shared display.
- **What is not established:** That a single guide-led forum causes lasting attitude change, reconciliation or civic action.[web:23]

### Peer knowledge exchange
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Each group checks a complementary trace, teaches its finding to others and combines pieces into an answer none held alone.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Investigate → explain → ask for clarification → integrate → guide verifies.
- **Question atoms that fit:** GENERATE, SOURCE CHECK, JUSTIFY, CORROBORATE, RETRIEVE.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Peer explanation and evidence integration are plausible; classroom jigsaw achievement findings are mixed across studies. **Strength/directness:** review of **indirect** classroom evidence. **Best-supported setting:** a small group with time to understand and check each piece.[web:31]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Unequal speaking, inaccurate peer teaching, status competition and poor fit for groups unable to separate and reconvene safely. Avoid assigning anyone a viewpoint because of identity or background.[web:31]
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Share sourced information, never assign a visitor to “represent” a harmed community.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Distinct printed source cards / accessible reproductions viewed in small groups.
- **What is not established:** An advantage over a guide explaining the same evidence during a short tour; more interaction is not automatically better.[web:31]

### Creative translation
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Produce an evidence sketch, bounded caption, then-and-now photograph or optional short written response; mark what is observed versus interpreted.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Look → select → make → annotate → check.
- **Question atoms that fit:** NOTICE, JUSTIFY, READ THE REPRESENTATION, COMPARE, REVISE.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Directed drawing can aid learning in studied instructional contexts. **Strength/directness:** substantial but **indirect** drawing evidence; other products are not validated by that drawing effect. **Best-supported setting:** an observable object or place and a precise, low-pressure prompt.[web:122]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Art-performance anxiety, time and access barriers, unsourced captions, prohibited photography. Do not demand creative expression about someone else’s suffering.
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Check image rights, consent and the site’s rules; make sharing optional and avoid aestheticising remains or atrocity.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Pencil sketch or short paper caption / optional permitted, annotated rephotograph.
- **What is not established:** That poems, photo prompts or caption labs achieve the meta-analysis’s drawing effects—or that a polished artifact means a historically sound interpretation.[web:122]

### Participatory mapping and spatial layering
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Align a dated view, mark a route or omission, or compare documented and lived meanings of a place.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Locate → align or mark → compare → identify map choices → qualify.
- **Question atoms that fit:** COMPARE, READ THE REPRESENTATION, CONTEXTUALIZE, PERSPECTIVE CHECK, UNCERTAINTY.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Community experiential mapping can surface meanings omitted by conventional distribution maps. **Strength/directness:** heritage **case-study** support, not a tested short-tour learning effect. **Best-supported setting:** place-based community fieldwork.[web:151]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** False precision, incompatible map scales or dates, uneven route access, exposing private or restricted locations.
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Do not solicit or publish sacred locations, private routines or vulnerable people’s routes without the relevant authority and consent; distinguish a visitor’s memory from an archival fact. Museum ethics require community involvement and consent for restricted traditional knowledge.[web:91]
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Rights-cleared paper base map or dated photo / optional correctly dated, labelled overlay.
- **What is not established:** That constructing a visitor map improves spatial-historical knowledge more than comparing well-sourced maps with a guide.[web:151]

### Skill, craft and systems demonstration
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Watch a skilled, bounded non-traumatic process; predict a consequential step; optionally try a safe replica action; explain a dependency or bottleneck.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Predict → observe or safely try → sequence → diagnose → debrief.
- **Question atoms that fit:** PREDICT, SEQUENCE, TEST, JUSTIFY, QUANTIFY / SCALE.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Live heritage interpretation supports encounters with objects and space; professional guidance establishes operational boundaries. **Strength/directness:** **direct but nonexperimental** live-interpretation evidence plus practice guidance, not a craft-trial effect. **Best-supported setting:** staffed living-history demonstrations.[web:71][web:142]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Injury, inaccurate technique, presenting one demonstrator’s reconstruction as universal, or confusing feasible replica work with an authentic past experience. No unsafe tools or untrained hands-on trial; safety takes precedence over authenticity.[web:142]
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Demonstrate non-traumatic technologies only when relevant; a craft activity must not purport to simulate forced labour, captivity or victim experience.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Skilled demonstration with a process diagram / optional short, accurately sourced close-up of an otherwise invisible step.
- **What is not established:** That a prediction pause, replica trial or bottleneck diagnosis independently improves retention on live tours.[web:71]

### Contribution and co-curation
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Submit a bounded transcription, evidence-based draft label, investigable question or optional local-knowledge lead for later review.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Produce → source → mark confidence → submit with consent → review.
- **Question atoms that fit:** GENERATE, SOURCE CHECK, JUSTIFY, UNCERTAINTY, PERSPECTIVE CHECK.
- **Educational purposes with support:** A reviewed workflow can produce accurate cultural-collection output. **Strength/directness:** empirical evidence for **online transcription output**, not educational impact or instant tour co-curation. **Best-supported setting:** institutions able to review and govern submissions.[web:81][web:76]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Unverified identifications, extractive collection of local knowledge, privacy and ownership disputes, implied promises that drafts will become official. Do not request a contribution without a real review and disposition pathway.
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Community authority, consent, acknowledgement and equitable treatment matter especially for sacred or Indigenous knowledge; some material should not be recorded or released.[web:91]
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** One authorised excerpt or draft-label sheet with clear collection rules / optional approved submission of a scan-linked transcription.
- **What is not established:** That micro-contributions improve visitor learning, or that a casual live submission reaches the accuracy of Smithsonian’s reviewed output.[web:81]

### Structured reflection
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** Record a provisional account privately; after encountering evidence, retain, qualify or change it and name the reason.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Commit privately → check → revise or retain → identify remaining question.
- **Question atoms that fit:** COMMIT, REVISE, JUSTIFY, UNCERTAINTY; **PREDICT → SHOW SOURCE → REVISE**.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Making a change in thinking articulable is the purpose of the Project Zero routine. **Strength/directness:** **framework**, with the proposed tour version a synthesis. **Best-supported setting:** a bounded inquiry with an evidentiary return.[web:139]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Treating change as the “right” outcome, public confession, leading before/after prompts and measuring expressed emotion as learning. Avoid forced disclosure of beliefs or trauma.
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Invite an evidence-based change in *interpretation*, not a declaration of personal moral transformation.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Private mental note or paper slip / optional private, non-shared response.
- **What is not established:** Improved retention, accurate belief revision or durable conduct change from a brief tour close.[web:139]

### Situated mixed-media inquiry
- **Core visitor experience / actions:** At the relevant location, compare an attributed recording, dated image or labelled overlay with what can—and cannot—be observed now.
- **Typical interaction atoms:** Source-check → listen or align → inspect place → corroborate → name limits.
- **Question atoms that fit:** SOURCE CHECK, COMPARE, READ THE REPRESENTATION, PERSPECTIVE CHECK, UNCERTAINTY.
- **Educational purposes with support:** Heritage-media literature supports the *possibility* of contextualised interpretation; a same-route tour study warns that managing audio can increase cognitive demand. **Strength/directness:** review plus **direct but nonrandom** tour comparison, neither a test of guide-led located inquiry. **Best-supported setting:** short, optional media comparisons anchored to a visible place.[web:47][web:10]
- **Known risks / contraindications:** Device distraction, inaccessible sound, inaccurate registration, reconstruction presented as fact, recording rights and unsafe headphone use while moving.
- **Sensitive-site considerations:** Obtain testimony rights; attribute speaker and date; offer an equally substantive non-audio route through the evidence; never use an invented voice as unattributed testimony.
- **Low-equipment / digital-enhanced:** Guide-held dated print or brief authorised reading / optional short recording or labelled overlay examined while stationary.
- **What is not established:** That adding AR or audio to live guiding improves objective learning, or that present fabric can “verify” every aspect of testimony.[web:47][web:10]

## Output 5 — Priority research gaps

1. **Short-tour efficacy:** Compare the same guide, route, evidence and duration with and without a *specific* visitor action—for example, a three-source dossier or evidence sketch. Assess warranted claims, not just enjoyment. Classroom intervention and drawing results cannot supply this estimate.[web:9][web:122]
2. **What survives a multi-stop walk:** Test whether visitors later recall a qualified claim and its source limits, rather than just the object or puzzle answer. Handling evidence presently separates object recall from broader knowledge.[web:13]
3. **Facilitation under real group conditions:** Observe whether brief jigsaws and deliberations produce accurate explanations and equitable participation when group size, noise and available time vary. Existing jigsaw results show material implementation risks.[web:31]
4. **Sensitive-site acceptability and harm:** Co-evaluate optional search, making, testimony and dialogue with site stewards and affected communities; record refusal, distress and perceived trivialisation as well as learning. This is a site-governance question, not permission to generalise Holocaust-specific rules everywhere.[web:46][web:91]
5. **Media’s incremental value:** On the *same live tour*, compare a short located print, audio or overlay against the same sourced information delivered without a device; measure attention to the actual place and accurate distinction between documentation and reconstruction. Existing AR reviews and audio-versus-guide comparisons do not answer this.[web:47][web:10]
6. **Contribution after the visit:** Determine whether live contributions are verified, consented to, useful and returned to contributors—and what review capacity they require. Accuracy of an established online transcription workflow is not evidence that an institution can responsibly accept tour contributions.[web:81][web:91]

### Strongly supported

**Within their original settings**, supported findings include improvement in source-based work after extended instruction; better object-related recall from permitted handling without reliable broad knowledge gains; variable rather than uniformly positive jigsaw outcomes; learning benefits from *directed drawing*; and accurate output from a reviewed transcription workflow. These are **not** strong evidence for the corresponding complete live-tour treatments.[web:9][web:13][web:31][web:122][web:81]

### Useful but indirect

Evidence investigation, object inquiry, bounded search, peer exchange, evidence sketching, mapping and situated media all have credible educational or interpretive work to do on a walk. Their expected outcomes should be stated narrowly—such as a better-qualified claim, a checked observation or a disclosed map omission—rather than promised as general learning or attitude gains.[web:9][web:13][web:28][web:31][web:122][web:151][web:47]

### Professional practice

Facilitated dialogue arcs, safe and accurately delimited craft demonstrations, private reflection routines, and consent-and-review pathways are usable professional structures. Their guidance is important precisely because an engaging experience alone does not settle safety, dignity, accuracy or community authority.[web:149][web:142][web:139][web:76][web:91]

### Speculative / needs testing

The **specific Run 01 live-guide recipes**—including a collective clue mosaic, diagnostic craft pause, counter-map, two-label workshop, before/after belief record and located testimony-and-place comparison—are informed syntheses. Their visitor actions are distinct and plausible, but no evidence above demonstrates that these exact short, guide-led formats produce their proposed outcomes. Confidence should attach to the tested component or applicable guidance, **not be borrowed wholesale for the new recipe**.

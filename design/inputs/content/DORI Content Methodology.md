# DORI Content Methodology

## From Research to Authoring System

## PHASE 1 — Research Constraints Audit

### What counts as each layer

The research (Runs 01–04) establishes nine distinct layers. Collapsing any two produces design errors.

**TREATMENT** — A recognizable visitor-experience structure with a beginning, middle and end. The visitor does something, encounters something, and exits changed. Examples: chronology reconstruction, evidence dossier, parallel voices, four corners, object mystery, detail hunt, dialogue arc. A treatment is not a topic, a content type, or a delivery medium.

**INTERACTION ATOM** — A reusable micro-action that appears inside many treatments. Atoms are verbs. Examples: NOTICE, SEARCH, SORT, SEQUENCE, RANK, MATCH, COMPARE, CHOOSE, COMMIT, PREDICT, TEST, REVISE, ALIGN, MAP, MOVE, CREATE, TEACH, PERFORM, TRACE, CONTRIBUTE, LISTEN, REFLECT, DECODE, COLLECT, PRODUCE. A treatment is typically 3–6 atoms in sequence.

**QUESTION ATOM** — A reusable cognitive prompt insertable inside almost any treatment. Questions are first-class primitives, not a treatment type. Examples: NOTICE, PREDICT, INFER, COMPARE, CHOOSE, SEQUENCE, RETRIEVE, CHALLENGE, SOURCE CHECK, UNCERTAINTY, BRIDGE, COMMIT, JUSTIFY, REVISE, CORROBORATE, CONTEXTUALIZE, TEST, READ THE REPRESENTATION, QUANTIFY/SCALE, PERSPECTIVE CHECK, GENERATE. Each has placement rules, branching logic, and content-form pairings.

**CONTENT FORM** — The raw material the treatment transforms. Examples: timeline, testimony, life story, quotation, primary document, map, photograph, object, statistic, tradition, oral history, archival film, reconstruction, sound, landscape, absence/vanished feature. A content form is NOT a treatment. The same testimony can become an evidence dossier, a parallel voices reading, a dialogue arc, or a structured reflection.

**GUIDE MOVE** — A concrete facilitation action. Examples: directed looking, revoice, wait, reveal, correct, anchor, bridge, pause, reconvene. Moves are what the guide does at a specific moment. A move is smaller than a treatment and larger than a word.

**GUIDE STANCE** — A behavioral principle that governs how the guide operates across all treatments. Examples: honest uncertainty, provocation not instruction, fair framing (raise relevant perspectives), verbatim attribution. A stance is not deployed at one moment — it is maintained throughout.

**CONSTRAINT** — A rule limiting what may be done. Constraints are not treatments and not stances — they are boundaries. Examples: no simulation of victims, graphic material is opt-in, no invented speech, no causation assertion between 2023 division and Oct 7, consent and rights for testimony, equal opt-out paths. Some constraints are universal (no simulation at memorial sites); some are context-dependent (puzzles are fine at ordinary heritage sites but require care at atrocity sites).

**RENDERER** — A physical or digital surface that presents content. Examples: guide voice, printed card, laminated photo, interactive HTML timeline, swipeable tablet cards, infographic, before/after slider, audio clip, video clip, annotated photograph, collapsible article, AR overlay, static map, interactive map. A renderer is NOT a treatment. An interactive timeline is a renderer; chronology reconstruction is the treatment it might render.

**TOUR-LEVEL / LOGISTICAL DESIGN** — Choices about the whole experience rather than one stop. Examples: food placement, drive pacing, closing ritual placement, guide rotation and debrief, security check timing. These are architecture, not content.

### Research cautions

**Persona → treatment matching:** The research explicitly warns against deterministic mapping. Do not say teens always get games or older adults get lecture. The strongest adaptation inputs are: stated purpose, demonstrated knowledge, specific questions, and observable attention — not demographics. Age, nationality, dress and religion are not supported as proxies for interests or values.

**Claims of effectiveness:** No treatment has been proven to improve learning outcomes on live heritage tours specifically. Evidence comes from classrooms (historical inquiry), museums (object handling, observation scaffolds), and online (transcription accuracy). Transfer is plausible but unproven. Label every claim with its actual evidence setting.

**Age stereotypes:** The research rejects fixed age-treatment assignments. Creative treatments work better for people in their 70s than no creativity at all. The principle: invest more creativity in younger groups, but never default to lecture for older ones.

**Timing rules:** No optimal number of questions per stop. No universal treatment duration. No fixed ratio of guide-talk to visitor-action. These remain professional judgment.

**Digital media:** Digital is a renderer, not pedagogy. Adding AR or audio to live guiding has no proven learning benefit. A same-route study warns that managing audio can increase cognitive demand. Digital supplements the guide — it never replaces them. Rule: show what cannot be seen from here, then return attention to the site.

**Sensitive / sacred / traumatic settings:** The constraint matrix is context-dependent. Puzzles and games are fine at ordinary heritage sites but require care at memorial/atrocity sites. Craft demonstrations are fine unless they simulate forced labor or captivity. Dialogue is fine unless it puts documented atrocities or human dignity to a vote. Do not apply Holocaust-specific guidance universally to all heritage.

**Provenance:** Stays attached to every factual claim and source. Attributed quotations remain verbatim. Reconstruction must be labeled as reconstruction. Traditions are attributed as traditions. Evidence status is preserved without suppressing creativity.

**Consent:** Testimony requires permission from archive and speaker. Visitor contribution does not imply permission to publish. Contribution requires a real review and disposition pathway.

**Accessibility:** Requirements must not be hidden for the sake of a reveal. Alternative paths must be genuinely equivalent.

**Visitor opt-in:** Participation must have genuine opt-out. Both paths (participate and observe) must be equal. Distressing material requires warning and choice before exposure, not during.

## PHASE 2A — Authoring Language

This is the vocabulary a content designer uses to build an experience. It must be small enough to learn in an hour, precise enough to prevent layer confusion, and generative enough to create treatment #81.

### The terms

**BIG IDEA** — The one interpretive claim this segment serves. Not a topic. Not a title. A statement a visitor could argue with. Example: "Israeli society entered October 2023 fractured and inward-facing." A big idea is falsifiable and interesting.

**OUTCOME** — What the visitor can do, say, or notice after the experience that they could not do before. Not a feeling. Not "understand." A testable capability. Example: "Describe two dimensions of the 2022–2023 civic conflict and distinguish chronological proximity from established causation."

**AHA** — The single shift in understanding that makes the experience worth having. The moment where the visitor's model updates. Example: "The country was arguing about its own rules — and nobody was watching the fence." An aha is earned, not announced.

**ANCHOR** — The specific site, object, view, sound, or dated source that grounds this experience in this place and not anywhere else. Without an anchor, the experience is a classroom lesson.

**TREATMENT** — The experience structure. What visitors do, what the guide does, what is withheld, what is revealed, and in what order. A treatment transforms content into experience.

**ASK** — Pose a question. Specify: which question atom, what is withheld until after, and what the guide does with each type of answer.

**SHOW** — Present a source, image, map, object, or view. Specify: what the visitor should notice, and what is not yet explained.

**DO** — A visitor action: sort, rank, search, move, draw, point, choose, commit. Specify: what they act on and what it produces.

**NOTICE** — Direct attention before explaining. "Look south. What do you see?" The guide waits.

**REVEAL** — Disclose withheld information after a question, prediction, or commitment has given it weight. A reveal without a prior question is just a fact. Specify: what was withheld and why the timing matters.

**BRANCH** — What the guide does differently depending on the visitor's response. Not fake branching where the guide gives the same speech regardless. Specify: response type → guide move.

**BRIDGE** — The sentence or question that connects this segment to the next. Carries a thread forward. Example: "At the next stop, look for the trace of what we just discussed."

**SOURCE** — Attribution. Who said it, when, where, what kind of document, what its evidence status is. Stays attached to every factual claim.

**CONSTRAINT** — What must not be done. Applies regardless of treatment choice. Named, not hidden in prose.

**WAIT** — The guide says nothing. Minimum 5 seconds. Silence belongs to the visitor.

**NEXT** — Move to the next block or segment. The experience is not over — it continues.

### What these terms are NOT

They are not lesson-plan language ("facilitator instructional activity"). They are not database field names (those come later). They are not script directions ("the guide says..."). They are author-facing design moves — the smallest set of verbs and nouns needed to compose an experience.

### Notation

An experience can be sketched in shorthand:

NOTICE → ASK (predict) → SHOW (source) → ASK (compare) → REVEAL → BRIDGE

or:

ASK (calibrate) → SHOW (timeline) → DO (sequence) → REVEAL (anchor date) → ASK (infer) → BRIDGE

This notation is the combination grammar. It generates treatments, not just retrieves them.

## PHASE 2B — Core Experience Block

Every experience — regardless of treatment — fills this universal envelope. The envelope holds metadata and constraints. The treatment-specific payload sits inside it.

### Universal envelope

**segment\_id** — unique slug

**big\_idea** — the interpretive claim this segment serves

**outcome** — what the visitor can do afterward

**aha** — the understanding shift

**anchor** — what grounds this in place (site, object, view, sound, dated source)

**treatment** — which treatment pattern is used (from the library)

**time\_character** — micro (<3min) / short (3–10min) / activity (10–25min) / extended (25min+)

**energy** — quiet / moderate / active

**social\_mode** — individual / pair / small group / whole group

**guide\_control** — guide-led / shared / visitor-led

**sources\[\]** — every factual claim with provenance and evidence status

**constraints\[\]** — named boundaries that apply (from the constraint library)

**bridge\_in** — how the visitor arrives at this segment (from what prior experience)

**bridge\_out** — the thread carried to the next segment

**emotional\_register\_at\_exit** — where the visitor should be: curious / unsettled / reflective / ready / relieved

### Treatment-specific payload

Each treatment defines its own required and optional content slots. These are NOT universal — they differ per treatment. Examples:

**Chronology reconstruction** requires: events\[\] (each with date, headline, why-it-matters, provenance, evidence status), an anchor event, an ordering problem, a question before reveal, a reveal, a bridge question.

**Parallel voices** requires: voices\[\] (each with identity label, speaker, perspective context, verbatim text, provenance), a shared event, a contrast dimension, reader assignment, comparison question, debrief question.

**Evidence dossier** requires: sources\[\] (each with type, content, provenance, what it establishes, what it cannot establish), a bounded question the dossier can address, a corroboration task, an uncertainty statement.

**Detail hunt** requires: search area, clues\[\] (each with location hint, what to find, what it connects to), a reconvene question, a guide synthesis.

**Dialogue arc** requires: a genuine open question, starter positions, turn-taking rules, factual anchors, a closing that does not force agreement.

The pattern library (Phase 3) specifies the content slots for every canonical treatment.

### Three UX modes from one block

The same experience block renders in three modes:

**AUTHOR / PREP MODE** — Rich. All sources, all branches, all background, all constraints, all alternative treatments. The content designer and the guide preparing for tomorrow see everything.

**GUIDE LIVE MODE** — Radical simplicity. The guide facing a group sees: SAY (one line), ASK (one question), SHOW (one image/map), DO (one instruction), WAIT (duration), REVEAL (one line), NEXT. Large text. No scrolling. No essay reading while facing people. Branches visible as tabs or swipe, not buried in prose.

**VISITOR / FOLLOW-UP MODE** — Rich optional exploration. Maps, sources, timeline, photographs, testimony, video, references, deeper context. Available on the tablet during the tour (opt-in) or sent as a follow-up link afterward. The visitor browses; the visitor is never tested.

Underlying content is the same across modes. Facts are not duplicated merely because the renderer changes. A source cited in prep mode appears in visitor mode with the same provenance.

## PHASE 3 — Treatment Pattern Library

Each card is compact but operational. A content designer reads the card and knows what material to insert. Cards are grouped by family.

### Family 1: Evidence Investigation

#### Evidence Dossier

**One-line experience:** Visitors assemble sourced traces into a bounded claim, then name what remains uncertain. **Best educational function:** Evidentiary reasoning, source literacy, bounded conclusions. **Visitor does:** Examine 3–5 sourced items. Sort by what each establishes. Identify conflicts. Propose a bounded conclusion. Name what is missing. **Guide does:** Distribute sources. ASK (source check). Correct misattributions. REVEAL additional source after first conclusion. ASK (revise or retain). **Required content slots:** sources\[\] (type, content, provenance, what-it-establishes, what-it-cannot), bounded\_question, corroboration\_task, uncertainty\_statement, reveal\_source. **Optional:** image, map, timeline anchor. **Interaction atoms:** SORT, SOURCE, COMPARE, COMMIT, REVISE. **Question atoms:** SOURCE CHECK, CORROBORATE, JUSTIFY, UNCERTAINTY. **Typical flow:** SHOW sources → DO (sort) → ASK (what do these establish?) → COMMIT → REVEAL (new source) → ASK (revise?) → BRIDGE. **Possible branches:** Strong answer → harder source. Misconception → supply anchor date. Silence → pair exchange first. **Reveal:** A source that changes, complicates, or confirms the initial conclusion. **Exit/bridge:** The remaining uncertainty carries to the next stop. **Possible renderers:** Printed source cards, tablet swipe cards, collapsible article, guide voice. **Risks:** Treating a complex issue as solvable in 10 minutes. Manufacturing doubt about established harm. **Evidence status:** Indirect (classroom historical inquiry); SYNTHESIS for live-guide version.

#### Provenance Audit

**One-line experience:** Visitors trace a quotation, image or claim back to its origin and assess what it actually supports. **Best educational function:** Source literacy, distinguishing assertion from evidence. **Visitor does:** Receive an unattributed or under-attributed claim. ASK: who said this? When? To whom? For what purpose? Check provided provenance card. **Guide does:** Present the claim stripped of context. WAIT. REVEAL provenance. ASK (does the source change the claim's weight?). **Required content slots:** claim\_text, stripped\_version, full\_provenance (author, date, audience, purpose, evidence\_status), comparison\_question. **Interaction atoms:** NOTICE, SOURCE, COMPARE, REVISE. **Question atoms:** SOURCE CHECK, CONTEXTUALIZE, PERSPECTIVE CHECK. **Typical flow:** SHOW (stripped claim) → ASK (what does this establish?) → REVEAL (provenance) → ASK (revise?) → BRIDGE. **Risks:** Embarrassing visitors who accepted the claim. Implying all sources are unreliable. **Evidence status:** FRAMEWORK (library science source analysis); SYNTHESIS for live guide.

#### Corroboration Ladder

**One-line experience:** Two accounts of the same event are compared — shared ground is identified, conflicts are sourced, the remainder is named as open. **Visitor does:** Read/hear two accounts. List what both say. List where they disagree. Propose what a third source would need to show. **Guide does:** Provide accounts. ASK (compare). Correct false equivalence. ASK (what third source would help?). **Required content slots:** account\_a (text, speaker, provenance), account\_b (text, speaker, provenance), shared\_ground\_prompt, conflict\_prompt, third\_source\_question. **Interaction atoms:** COMPARE, SOURCE, CORROBORATE. **Question atoms:** COMPARE, CORROBORATE, UNCERTAINTY, GENERATE. **Typical flow:** SHOW (account A) → SHOW (account B) → ASK (compare) → DO (list shared/conflicting) → ASK (what else do we need?) → BRIDGE. **Risks:** Forcing agreement. Treating two accounts as equally informed when evidence clearly supports one. **Evidence status:** FRAMEWORK (historical inquiry); SYNTHESIS.

### Family 2: Ordering and Classification

#### Chronology Reconstruction

**One-line experience:** Visitors order events, discover their assumptions, and test them against dated evidence. **Visitor does:** Receive event cards without dates. Sort into guessed order. COMMIT. Receive anchor date. REVISE. **Guide does:** Distribute cards. WAIT. ASK (why that order?). REVEAL anchor date. ASK (what changes?). **Required content slots:** events\[\] (id, headline, short\_description, actual\_date, why\_it\_matters, provenance), anchor\_event (the dated evidence that tests the ordering), ordering\_problem (what assumption the sequence challenges), question\_before\_reveal, reveal, bridge\_question. **Optional:** image per event, timeline renderer. **Interaction atoms:** SEQUENCE, COMMIT, PREDICT, REVISE. **Question atoms:** SEQUENCE, PREDICT, INFER, COMPARE. **Typical flow:** DO (sequence) → COMMIT → ASK (why?) → REVEAL (anchor date) → ASK (revise?) → BRIDGE. **Possible renderers:** Printed cards, tablet swipe, interactive timeline, guide narration. **Risks:** Treating adjacent dates as proof of causation. Trick reveals based on information nobody had. **Evidence status:** Indirect (classroom chronological reasoning); PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE.

#### Card Sort / Priority Ranking

**One-line experience:** Visitors rank values, concepts or priorities, then discover what their ranking reveals about their assumptions. **Visitor does:** Receive cards. Rank by importance or eliminate by rounds. Explain top/bottom choice. **Guide does:** Distribute. WAIT. ASK (why is that one first?). Introduce a complication. ASK (would you change?). **Required content slots:** items\[\] (word or image, short context), ranking\_prompt, complication (new information that tests the ranking), debrief\_question. **Interaction atoms:** RANK, CHOOSE, COMMIT, REVISE. **Question atoms:** CHOOSE, JUSTIFY, CHALLENGE. **Typical flow:** DO (rank) → COMMIT → ASK (justify) → SHOW (complication) → ASK (revise?) → BRIDGE. **Risks:** Implying there is one correct ranking. Using as a personality test. **Evidence status:** PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE (informal education).

### Family 3: Narrative and Multiple-Perspective Structures

#### Parallel Voices

**One-line experience:** Multiple sourced perspectives on the same event are encountered in juxtaposition. No voice is declared right. **Visitor does:** Listen to or read 2–4 attributed voices. Identify where they agree and where they differ. Name which voice is closest to their own position. **Guide does:** Read/play voices. ASK (compare). Do not declare a winner. ASK (connection question). BRIDGE. **Required content slots:** voices\[\] (identity\_label, speaker\_name, perspective\_context, verbatim\_text, provenance, evidence\_status), shared\_event, contrast\_dimension, comparison\_question, debrief\_question. **Interaction atoms:** LISTEN, COMPARE, CHOOSE, REFLECT. **Question atoms:** COMPARE, PERSPECTIVE CHECK, CONTEXTUALIZE. **Typical flow:** SHOW (voice A) → SHOW (voice B) → ASK (compare) → ASK (which is closest to you?) → WAIT → BRIDGE. **Risks:** Implying exactly two sides. Using fictional voices for real events. Forcing visitors to disclose a position. **Evidence status:** PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE (Sites of Conscience, heritage interpretation).

#### Single-Person Thread

**One-line experience:** One named, documented person is followed through a timeline slot. The specificity is the experience. **Visitor does:** Listen to the person's documented story at this site. Notice details. Carry the thread to the next stop if the person's story continues. **Guide does:** Narrate from sourced material. Use the person's name, age, specific details. ASK (notice). BRIDGE to next stop where the thread continues. **Required content slots:** person (name, age, location, relationship\_to\_event, sourced\_details, provenance, consent\_status), timeline\_slot (start\_time, end\_time, what\_happened), key\_quote (verbatim, sourced), continuation (does the thread carry to the next stop?). **Interaction atoms:** LISTEN, NOTICE, TRACE. **Question atoms:** NOTICE, SEQUENCE, BRIDGE. **Risks:** Inventing details. Using a person without consent. Turning a life into a parable. **Evidence status:** PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE.

#### Reversal

**One-line experience:** Start with the outcome, then walk backward to reconstruct the cause. **Visitor does:** See the aftermath. ASK: what do you think happened here? Guide walks backward through the documented sequence. **Guide does:** Present outcome. ASK (predict). Narrate backward. ASK (at what point could the outcome have changed?). BRIDGE. **Required content slots:** outcome (what is visible now), documented\_sequence\[\] (steps backward, each with provenance), pivot\_question (the moment the sequence could have diverged). **Interaction atoms:** PREDICT, SEQUENCE, REVISE. **Question atoms:** PREDICT, INFER, CHALLENGE. **Evidence status:** SYNTHESIS (narrative technique adapted for heritage).

### Family 4: Sensory and Measured Encounter

#### Scale and Distance

**One-line experience:** A number becomes physical. The visitor's body measures what a statistic cannot convey. **Visitor does:** Look at a reference point. Hear the distance or time. Count it. Feel it. **Guide does:** "The fence was 800 meters from here. That building is 800 meters." Or: "You have 15 seconds. Count in your head." WAIT. BRIDGE. **Required content slots:** measurement (number, unit, what-it-measures, provenance), reference\_point (what the visitor can see), comparison (what makes the number meaningful). **Interaction atoms:** NOTICE, COMPARE. **Question atoms:** QUANTIFY/SCALE, COMPARE. **Typical flow:** NOTICE (reference) → SHOW (measurement) → ASK (compare) → WAIT → BRIDGE. **Risks:** Gamifying a serious number. Using an inaccurate comparison. **Evidence status:** PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE.

#### Sensory Mapping

**One-line experience:** An idea is connected to the five senses in sequence — what you see, hear, smell, touch, taste here. **Visitor does:** Attend to each sense at the site. Name what each sense registers. Connect sensory detail to the interpretive theme. **Guide does:** Direct attention sense by sense. ASK (what do you hear?). Connect answers to the big idea. BRIDGE. **Required content slots:** sensory\_prompts\[\] (sense, what-to-attend-to, connection-to-theme), big\_idea\_link. **Interaction atoms:** NOTICE, LISTEN, REFLECT. **Question atoms:** NOTICE, COMPARE, BRIDGE. **Evidence status:** PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE (informal education, Havdalah model).

### Family 5: Spatial Commitment and Argument

#### Four Corners

**One-line experience:** Visitors walk to a position that represents their stance, then defend it and hear others. **Visitor does:** Hear a dilemma with 4 positions. Walk to their corner. Explain why. Listen to other corners. Optionally move. **Guide does:** State the dilemma. WAIT for movement. ASK (why are you here?). Invite one voice per corner. ASK (anyone want to move?). BRIDGE. **Required content slots:** dilemma (sourced, genuine, no right answer), positions\[\] (4 labeled stances, each defensible), debrief\_question. **Interaction atoms:** CHOOSE, COMMIT, MOVE, LISTEN, REVISE. **Question atoms:** CHOOSE, JUSTIFY, PERSPECTIVE CHECK, CHALLENGE. **Typical flow:** SHOW (dilemma) → DO (move) → ASK (justify) → LISTEN (other corners) → ASK (revise?) → BRIDGE. **Risks:** Exposing minority positions in an unsafe group. Using at memorial sites where a stance trivializes harm. **Constraint:** Never at a memorial stop. Only at framing sessions or in the van (adapted as show-of-hands). **Evidence status:** PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE (informal education, Noar Telem).

#### Floor Scale / Spectrum

**One-line experience:** Visitors position themselves on a physical spectrum between two poles. **Visitor does:** Hear two poles. Stand where they fall on the line. Explain their position to their neighbor. **Guide does:** Define the poles. WAIT. ASK someone in the middle (why there?). ASK someone at an extreme. BRIDGE. **Required content slots:** pole\_a (label, sourced position), pole\_b (label, sourced position), spectrum\_question, complication (new information that might shift positions). **Interaction atoms:** CHOOSE, COMMIT, MOVE, COMPARE. **Question atoms:** CHOOSE, JUSTIFY, COMPARE. **Constraint:** Same as four corners — framing session only, not at memorial stops. **Evidence status:** PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE.

### Family 6: Dialogic Deliberation

#### Dialogue Arc

**One-line experience:** Visitors work through a genuine question with turn-taking, factual anchors, and a close that does not force agreement. **Visitor does:** Hear a framing question. Share an initial response. Listen to others. Encounter a factual anchor. Revise or hold position. Name what remains open. **Guide does:** Frame the question. Manage turns. Introduce factual anchors at moments of drift. ASK (what changed?). Close with uncertainty, not consensus. **Required content slots:** framing\_question (genuine, open, sourced context), factual\_anchors\[\] (evidence introduced mid-dialogue), turn\_rules (how many speak, for how long), closing\_question. **Interaction atoms:** LISTEN, COMMIT, COMPARE, REVISE, REFLECT. **Question atoms:** PERSPECTIVE CHECK, CHALLENGE, JUSTIFY, UNCERTAINTY. **Typical flow:** ASK (frame) → LISTEN → SHOW (anchor) → ASK (revise?) → LISTEN → BRIDGE. **Risks:** Dominant voices. Putting documented harm to a vote. Fake consensus. **Constraint:** Distinguish factual claims from policy trade-offs. Do not deliberate whether an atrocity happened. **Evidence status:** FRAMEWORK (Sites of Conscience facilitated dialogue); PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE.

#### Aquarium Debate

**One-line experience:** Two visitors argue assigned positions (not their own). Others observe, then tap in. **Visitor does:** Receive an assigned position with sourced evidence. Argue it. Observers watch, then replace a debater. **Guide does:** Assign positions. Provide evidence cards. Manage tap-ins. ASK (what was the strongest argument you heard?). BRIDGE. **Required content slots:** position\_a (sourced stance, evidence), position\_b (sourced stance, evidence), tap\_in\_rules, debrief\_question. **Interaction atoms:** PERFORM, LISTEN, COMPARE, TEACH. **Question atoms:** JUSTIFY, CHALLENGE, PERSPECTIVE CHECK. **Risks:** Positions must be sourced, not invented. Not suitable for all group dynamics. **Evidence status:** PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE.

### Family 7: Playful Search and Puzzle

#### Detail Hunt

**One-line experience:** Visitors search a bounded area for specific evidence, then reconvene to assemble what they found. **Visitor does:** Receive a search brief (what to look for, where to look). Search. Find clues. Report back. **Guide does:** Distribute briefs. Set boundary and time. ASK (what did you find?). Synthesize. REVEAL what was missed. BRIDGE. **Required content slots:** search\_area (boundaries, safety), clues\[\] (location\_hint, what\_to\_find, what\_it\_connects\_to, provenance), reconvene\_question, guide\_synthesis. **Interaction atoms:** SEARCH, NOTICE, COLLECT, TEACH. **Question atoms:** NOTICE, INFER, SOURCE CHECK. **Typical flow:** SHOW (brief) → DO (search) → RECONVENE → ASK (what did you find?) → REVEAL (missed items) → BRIDGE. **Risks:** Speed over evidence. Unsafe areas. Trivializing a serious site with game mechanics. **Constraint:** At memorial/atrocity sites, only sober trace-searches, never scored or timed. **Evidence status:** PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE (museum hunts); no objective learning evidence.

#### Evidence Mystery

**One-line experience:** A bounded unknown is investigated through sourced clues, leading to a qualified conclusion. **Visitor does:** Receive a case file with 3–5 clues. Propose hypotheses. Test against additional evidence. Reach a bounded conclusion. **Guide does:** Present the mystery. Distribute clues in stages. ASK (what does this establish?). REVEAL discriminating evidence. ASK (revise?). BRIDGE. **Required content slots:** mystery\_question (bounded, answerable from evidence), clues\[\] (staged, each with provenance), discriminating\_evidence (the clue that resolves or narrows), conclusion\_prompt, uncertainty\_statement. **Interaction atoms:** SEARCH, DECODE, PREDICT, TEST, REVISE. **Question atoms:** INFER, SOURCE CHECK, CORROBORATE, UNCERTAINTY. **Risks:** Unsolvable mystery. Trick reveal. Manufacturing doubt about established facts. **Evidence status:** SYNTHESIS (escape-room and inquiry learning adapted).

### Family 8: Object and Process Inquiry

#### Object Mystery

**One-line experience:** A material thing is examined without its label. Visitors infer function, then check against documented evidence. **Visitor does:** Handle or examine a safe object/replica. Describe features. Propose function. Check against provenance card. **Guide does:** Present object without context. ASK (what do you notice?). ASK (what might this be for?). REVEAL provenance. ASK (what changes?). BRIDGE. **Required content slots:** object (description, safe\_handling\_notes), stripped\_presentation (no label), observation\_prompts\[\], provenance\_card (name, date, function, source), comparison\_object (optional). **Interaction atoms:** NOTICE, PREDICT, TEST, REVISE. **Question atoms:** NOTICE, INFER, JUSTIFY, SOURCE CHECK. **Constraint:** Handle only authorized originals or labeled replicas. No sacred objects without authority. No traumatic implements as mystery props. **Evidence status:** INDIRECT (museum object-handling recall study); SYNTHESIS for live guide.

### Family 9: Peer Knowledge Exchange

#### Jigsaw / Distributed Investigation

**One-line experience:** Different visitors hold complementary sources. Each investigates theirs, then teaches the others. Nobody holds the whole picture alone. **Visitor does:** Receive one source from a set. Analyze it. Present findings to the group. Listen to others. Synthesize. **Guide does:** Distribute different sources. Set analysis task. Manage report-backs. VERIFY each group's claims. Synthesize. ASK (what do all sources together establish?). BRIDGE. **Required content slots:** sources\[\] (complementary, each with provenance, each answering a different part of the question), analysis\_prompt, report\_format, synthesis\_question. **Interaction atoms:** SOURCE, TEACH, LISTEN, COMPARE, COLLECT. **Question atoms:** SOURCE CHECK, COMPARE, CORROBORATE, GENERATE. **Risks:** One unchecked peer account becoming group fact. Unequal source quality. **Constraint:** Guide verifies every peer explanation before the group moves on. **Evidence status:** INDIRECT (classroom jigsaw meta-analysis, highly variable effects); SYNTHESIS.

### Family 10: Creative Translation

#### Evidence Sketch / Caption Lab

**One-line experience:** Looking culminates in a visitor-authored artifact — a sketch, caption, or photograph — that records what can actually be observed. **Visitor does:** Observe an object, view, or source closely. Draw, caption, or photograph it. Annotate what is observation versus interpretation. Share (optional). **Guide does:** Provide prompt. ASK (what are you recording?). Check observation/interpretation distinction. Invite (not require) sharing. BRIDGE. **Required content slots:** observation\_target (what to look at), prompt (what to record), materials (paper, pencil, phone), annotation\_guide (label what you see vs. what you infer). **Interaction atoms:** NOTICE, CREATE, SOURCE. **Question atoms:** NOTICE, INFER, JUSTIFY. **Constraint:** Optional expression. ## PHASE 4 — Embedded Question System

Questions are first-class primitives. They are not a treatment. They live inside treatments. Every treatment card specifies which question atoms fit and where they go.

### Canonical question atoms (21)

**NOTICE** — "What do you see / hear / smell here?" Grounds observation before inference. Best placement: before explanation, on arrival. Withhold: the label, the name, the function. Guide follow-up: build from what they noticed; point at what they missed.

**PREDICT** — "What do you expect to find / happen next?" Creates a testable expectation. Best placement: before a reveal, before a demonstration, during a story. Withhold: the outcome. Guide follow-up: show the outcome; compare.

**INFER** — "What might this mean? What could explain it?" Moves from observation to interpretation. Best placement: after observation, after a detail is noticed. Withhold: the established explanation. Guide follow-up: ask for evidence ("what makes you say that?"); then reveal.

**COMPARE** — "How are these different? What do both show?" Juxtaposes two items to reveal a distinction or shared ground. Best placement: after two sources/views/objects are presented. Withhold: the guide's synthesis. Guide follow-up: name shared ground and conflict.

**CHOOSE** — "Which source / explanation / path do you want to pursue?" Forces a genuine selection with consequences. Best placement: after options are presented. Withhold: what the unchosen option would have shown (reveal later). Guide follow-up: follow the chosen path; then disclose the other.

**SEQUENCE** — "What came first? What order?" Tests chronological assumptions. Best placement: before anchor dates are revealed. Withhold: the correct order. Guide follow-up: reveal anchor date; ask what changes.

**RETRIEVE** — "What did we learn at the last stop that applies here?" Connects earlier content to the present. Best placement: on arrival at a new stop. Withhold: the connection. Guide follow-up: if they retrieve it, build on it; if not, supply it and bridge.

**CHALLENGE** — "What would disprove this? What counterevidence exists?" Tests a conclusion's robustness. Best placement: after a committed answer. Withhold: the counterexample. Guide follow-up: present the counterexample; ask to revise.

**SOURCE CHECK** — "Who said this? When? To whom? For what purpose?" Traces a claim to its origin. Best placement: after a quotation, statistic, or image is shown. Withhold: the full provenance. Guide follow-up: reveal provenance; ask if it changes the weight.

**UNCERTAINTY** — "What can't we know from this? What's missing?" Names the limit of what evidence establishes. Best placement: after a conclusion, after a source comparison. Withhold: nothing — this question opens, not closes. Guide follow-up: carry the uncertainty as a bridge to the next stop.

**BRIDGE** — "At the next stop, look for..." Carries a thread forward. Best placement: at transition between segments. Withhold: the answer (payoff at the next stop). Guide follow-up: on arrival, ask if they found it.

**COMMIT** — "Write down / note your answer before we continue." Makes a position visible (even privately) so it can be revisited. Best placement: before evidence that might change the answer. Withhold: the disconfirming evidence. Guide follow-up: after evidence, ask to revise or retain.

**JUSTIFY** — "What makes you say that? Which evidence?" Requests a warrant, not just an answer. Best placement: after any answer. Withhold: nothing. Guide follow-up: examine the cited evidence; confirm or correct.

**REVISE** — "Has anything changed? Would you adjust your answer?" Invites updating after new evidence. Best placement: after a reveal. Withhold: the "right" answer — revision is self-directed. Guide follow-up: acknowledge the change or the justified stability.

**CORROBORATE** — "Does the second source support or conflict with the first?" Tests agreement across sources. Best placement: after two sources. Withhold: a third source. Guide follow-up: introduce the third source or name what remains open.

**CONTEXTUALIZE** — "Who was the audience? What was the occasion? What year?" Places a source in its historical setting. Best placement: after a quotation or dramatic reading. Withhold: the full context. Guide follow-up: reveal context; ask how it changes the reading.

**TEST** — "How could we check this? What would we expect to see?" Proposes a way to verify. Best placement: after a hypothesis. Withhold: the test result. Guide follow-up: perform or describe the test.

**READ THE REPRESENTATION** — "What does the key say? What units? Whose data?" Examines how a display (map, infographic, reconstruction) was constructed. Best placement: before interpreting any visual representation. Withhold: the author's choices (scale, omissions, framing). Guide follow-up: compare with an alternative representation.

**QUANTIFY / SCALE** — "How many? Over what period? Compared to what?" Turns a dramatic number into a properly scaled claim. Best placement: after a statistic is presented. Withhold: the denominator or comparison. Guide follow-up: supply the denominator.

**PERSPECTIVE CHECK** — "Whose view is this? Who is not represented?" Identifies the position from which a source speaks. Best placement: after testimony, after a map, after a narrative. Withhold: nothing. Guide follow-up: name who is absent and why.

**GENERATE** — "What question would you ask? What would you want to investigate?" Visitor produces the next inquiry. Best placement: at the end of a segment, or when visitor curiosity should drive the next step. Withhold: nothing. Guide follow-up: if evidence permits, follow the question; if not, carry it.

### Placement rules (from research)

Before explanation — surface a detail or initial model first. During a story — interrupt at a consequential decision, not an arbitrary sentence. Before a reveal — make an expectation testable. After viewing an object — move from observation to inference and limits. After source comparison — test interpretation against agreement and conflict. During transition — turn travel time into a forward-looking thread. After a committed choice — let the choice have interpretive consequence. After a misconception — locate the reasoning and supply discriminating evidence.

### Branching table (from research)

Correct with evidence → confirm, advance to harder implication. Correct without reason → invite light justification before moving on. Plausible but unsupported → revoice as hypothesis, ask what would test it. Misconception → identify the claim, provide correction with evidence, offer chance to revise. Disagreement within group → ask each side for strongest evidence, compare sources. Silence → allow time, shift to accessible detail or pair exchange, then continue. Unexpectedly sophisticated → acknowledge, invite concise explanation, reconnect group. Unexpected interest → follow briefly if evidence and time permit; carry to a later stop if not.

### What makes a question weak

A question is weak if the guide gives exactly the same speech regardless of the answer. Design for: visitor response → guide response. If every path leads to the same next paragraph, the question was decoration, not a thinking prompt.

### Content-Form Transformation Matrix

The same content can become many experiences. This matrix shows how.

**Timeline** — Weak/default: display as a page. Possible treatments: chronology reconstruction, reversal, parallel lives, evidence dossier, corroboration ladder. Useful atoms: SEQUENCE, COMPARE, PREDICT. Useful questions: SEQUENCE, INFER, COMPARE ("which change came first, and does order establish causation?"). Renderers: interactive timeline, printed cards, swipeable tablet, guide narration.

**Testimony** — Weak/default: play full clip as atmosphere. Possible treatments: single-person thread, parallel voices, corroboration ladder, dialogue arc, located comparison. Useful atoms: LISTEN, COMPARE, SOURCE. Useful questions: SOURCE CHECK, PERSPECTIVE CHECK, CORROBORATE ("what did this speaker witness firsthand, and what are they reporting secondhand?"). Renderers: audio clip, testimony card, guide reading, video with subtitles.

**Life story** — Weak/default: read a biography aloud. Possible treatments: single-person thread, chronology reconstruction, evidence dossier. Useful atoms: LISTEN, TRACE, NOTICE. Useful questions: SEQUENCE, CONTEXTUALIZE, PERSPECTIVE CHECK. Renderers: testimony card, article, guide narration.

**Photograph** — Weak/default: show as illustration. Possible treatments: detail hunt, provenance audit, evidence sketch, located comparison, before/after. Useful atoms: NOTICE, SEARCH, ALIGN. Useful questions: NOTICE, INFER, SOURCE CHECK, READ THE REPRESENTATION ("what is visible outside the frame; what can't this tell us?"). Renderers: printed image, tablet, before/after slider, annotated photograph.

**Map** — Weak/default: point at locations. Possible treatments: counter-map, located comparison, chronology reconstruction, evidence dossier, detail hunt. Useful atoms: MAP, SEARCH, COMPARE. Useful questions: READ THE REPRESENTATION, COMPARE, PERSPECTIVE CHECK ("which path appears on the older map but not the new one?"). Renderers: static map, interactive map, printed overlay, guide narration.

**Object** — Weak/default: state name, date, function. Possible treatments: object mystery, evidence sketch, process demonstration. Useful atoms: NOTICE, TEST, PREDICT. Useful questions: NOTICE, INFER, JUSTIFY, SOURCE CHECK. Renderers: physical object/replica, photograph, guide handling.

**Quotation** — Weak/default: read a striking line without context. Possible treatments: parallel voices, provenance audit, contested text study. Useful atoms: LISTEN, SOURCE, COMPARE. Useful questions: SOURCE CHECK, CONTEXTUALIZE, PERSPECTIVE CHECK. Renderers: guide voice, printed card, tablet.

**Statistic** — Weak/default: announce a dramatic number. Possible treatments: scale and distance, evidence dossier, system bottleneck. Useful atoms: COMPARE, TRACE, TEST. Useful questions: QUANTIFY/SCALE, SOURCE CHECK, CHALLENGE ("what population and period does this figure cover?"). Renderers: infographic, guide narration, printed card.

Useful atoms: DECODE, TEACH, SOURCE. Useful questions: SOURCE CHECK, JUSTIFY, CORROBORATE, UNCERTAINTY. Renderers: source viewer, printed card, guide reading.

**Reconstruction** — Weak/default: present design as excavated fact. Possible treatments: system bottleneck, evidence dossier, located comparison. Useful atoms: TEST, COMPARE, REVISE. Useful questions: READ THE REPRESENTATION, CHALLENGE, UNCERTAINTY. Renderers: model, image overlay, annotated photograph.

### Renderer Library

Renderers are surfaces, not pedagogy. The same treatment can be rendered multiple ways.

**Guide voice** — Good at: immediacy, adaptation, emotional register, responding to the group. Bad at: showing detail, persisting after the tour. Supports: all content forms. Risks: attention fatigue after 3 minutes; lecture mode.

**Printed card** — Good at: hands-on, tangible, sortable, no batteries. Bad at: updates, multimedia, accessibility for low-vision visitors. Supports: events, quotations, sources, sorting tasks. Risks: litter, loss, rain.

**Infographic (tablet)** — Good at: making one number land in under 30 seconds. Bad at: depth, narrative, interactivity. Supports: statistics, comparisons, distances. Risks: too small for groups >4.

**Interactive timeline (tablet)** — Good at: showing sequence, allowing exploration, zooming between scales. Bad at: replacing the guide's narration, group viewing. Supports: timelines, chronology reconstruction. Risks: device distraction; visitors browse instead of listening.

**Before/after slider (tablet)** — Good at: visceral comparison of change. Bad at: nuance, context. Supports: photographs, maps, landscapes. Risks: without provenance labels, implies a simple decline narrative.

**Testimony card (tablet)** — Good at: presenting one person with dignity — name, photo, quote, optional audio. Bad at: comparison, depth beyond the card. Supports: testimony, life story. Risks: without consent badge, implies unrestricted use.

**Collapsible article (tablet/phone)** — Good at: depth the visitor chooses. Bad at: competing with the site for attention. Supports: all content forms for follow-up. Risks: reading during a live segment.

**Audio clip** — Good at: first-person voice, emotional register. Bad at: group listening outdoors, accessibility for deaf/HoH visitors. Supports: testimony, oral history, sound. Risks: cognitive load with guide narration; must have non-audio equivalent.

**Static map (printed or tablet)** — Good at: orientation, comparison, annotation. Bad at: interactivity, layering. Supports: maps, spatial tasks.

**Interactive map (tablet)** — Good at: toggle layers, zoom, tap-for-detail. Bad at: replacing on-site looking. Supports: participatory mapping, located comparison. Risks: device replaces the landscape.

**Image overlay (tablet)** — Good at: aligning historical image with present view. Bad at: accuracy depends on exact vantage. Supports: located comparison, before/after. Risks: misregistration presented as fact.

**Object / replica** — Good at: tactile engagement, mystery, prediction. Bad at: scale, conservation. Supports: object mystery, process demonstration. Risks: damage, loss, sacred-object prohibitions.

## PHASE 6 — Combination Grammar

Recipes are not treatments. They are atom sequences that generate treatments when filled with content. This is the generative layer — it creates treatment #81.

### Core recipes (from research)

**SEARCH → CLUE → INFER → REVEAL** — Find a site clue, propose meaning, check sourced context. Good for: fabric, landscape, object. Common failure: speed over evidence. Constraint: safe boundary; answer supported by the clue.

**NOTICE → PREDICT → SHOW → COMPARE** — Make a fair expectation; inspect what the next source changes. Good for: object, video, photograph, process. Common failure: trick reveal based on information nobody had.

**SOURCE → CHOOSE → DEFEND → CHALLENGE** — Prioritize a source or explanation, then test its warrant. Good for: primary source, quotation, plan. Common failure: fake choice where all paths lead to the same speech. Constraint: genuine option; correct unequal evidential support.

**STORY → PAUSE → PREDICT → REVEAL** — Follow a documented beat, pause, check the next beat. Good for: life story, testimony, letter. Common failure: turning hardship into entertainment. Constraint: no invented motive.

**ROLE → PERFORM → COMPARE → DISCUSS** — Act as a present-day investigator or perform a safe craft step; check the result. Good for: replica, process, case file. Common failure: impersonating victims. Constraint: never impersonate victims; competent supervision; label reconstruction.

**MAP → SEARCH → SCALE → REVEAL** — Find a mapped feature, check scale/key, compare with located evidence. Good for: map, landscape, vanished route. Common failure: ignoring differing scales. Constraint: date, scale, access, sensitive-location controls.

**SOURCE CHECK → CORROBORATE → UNCERTAINTY** — Build a bounded claim while distinguishing dependent accounts. Good for: testimony, dossiers, records. Common failure: manufacturing doubt about established harm.

**NOTICE → INFER → JUSTIFY → TEST** — Turn a visible feature into an explanation that could fail. Good for: object, fabric, photograph. Common failure: stating an inferred use as certain.

**PREDICT → DEMONSTRATE → COMPARE → DEBRIEF** — A material outcome checks a proposed mechanism. Good for: safe craft, replica, system model. Common failure: reconstruction treated as past experience.

**SEARCH → COLLECT → TEACH → VERIFY** — Different peers assemble a finding no one initially holds alone. Good for: complementary records, site traces. Common failure: unverified peer explanation becomes fact. Constraint: guide verifies.

**NOTICE → CREATE → ANNOTATE → VERIFY** — Make an evidence-bearing sketch or caption. Good for: object, photograph, fabric. Common failure: confusing observation with interpretation.

**ALIGN → COMPARE → READ THE REPRESENTATION → QUALIFY** — Register an older depiction with the place, then examine mismatch. Good for: dated photo, map, reconstruction. Common failure: misregistration presented as fact.

**STORY → CHOOSE SOURCE → INVESTIGATE → RECONVENE** — Choose an equivalent line of inquiry, exchange findings. Good for: attributed account, paired source sets. Common failure: choice over a past person's fate.

**COMMIT PRIVATELY → SHOW SOURCE → REVISE OR RETAIN** — Make the reason for change visible. Good for: reconstruction, map, bounded source set. Common failure: rewarding reversal.

**BRIDGE → FIND TRACE → NAME LIMIT** — Carry a promised question to another stop and pay it off. Good for: landscape, map, absence. Common failure: losing the promised thread.

**LISTEN → SOURCE CHECK → PLACE COMPARE → NAME LIMIT** — Hear authorized testimony without treating the present fabric as its full verification. Good for: testimony, oral history, landscape. Common failure: invented voice as unattributed testimony.

### How to use the grammar

1. Start with your content form (what you have).
2. Look up that form in the transformation matrix.
3. Pick the atoms that fit the educational target.
4. Sequence them into a recipe.
5. Insert question atoms at the recommended positions.
6. Specify what is withheld and when it is revealed.
7. Write the branch: what the guide does with each type of answer.
8. Choose a renderer.
9. Check constraints.

The grammar does not require you to use a named treatment from the library. If the recipe works and the constraints hold, it is a valid treatment.

## PHASE 7 — Worked Example: Tel Aviv Drive

Applying the full methodology to Stop 0.

### Experience Block (universal envelope)

segment\_id: drive-tel-aviv-2022-2023

outcome: Visitors can describe important dimensions of the 2022–2023 civic conflict and distinguish "this was happening beforehand" from "this caused what happened afterward."

aha: Chronological proximity is not established causation. The country was arguing about its own rules — and the question of who was watching the borders was not part of the public argument.

ASK (calibrate) → SHOW (timeline, guide narration + tablet) → ASK (predict: what order?) → REVEAL (anchor date) → SHOW (voice A, voice B) → ASK (compare) → ASK (provocation) → NOTICE (landscape) → ASK (bridge/unanswered) → WAIT → BRIDGE (opt-in)

**Q1 — Calibration (minute 2):** "What do you already know about what was happening in Israel in 2023?" Purpose: discover prior knowledge. Withhold: nothing — this is a calibration. **Q4 — Connection (minute 20):** "Does this sound familiar? Is there a version of this in your country?" Purpose: make it personal. Withhold: nothing. Branch: if they engage, follow. If not, move on.

**Q5 — Unanswered (minute 27):** "Nobody I have spoken to — not the soldiers, not the residents, not the intelligence people — expected what happened the next morning." Purpose: open a gap the rest of the day fills. Withhold: everything about Oct 7. Branch: silence. The guide says nothing. The landscape speaks.

Withheld until after Q2: the actual dates of the protests and the vote. Withheld until after Q3: any guide commentary on which side had the stronger argument (never revealed — the guide does not take a side). Withheld until arrival at first field stop: everything about October 7 itself. Revealed at Q5: the emotional weight of the silence. Not information — presence.

### Branching: visitors who know almost nothing

Give the full spine slowly, with the infographic (5 elections) and the interactive timeline on the tablet. Ask Q2 after the events are presented. Give both voices. Ask Q3. Spend less time on Q4 (they may not have a reference point). Q5 lands the same way for everyone.

## WHAT WE SHOULD LOCK NOW

### Israeli Mosaic stereotype-copy register

For Israeli Mosaic lens reveals, the `YOU KNOW THE TYPE…` side must sound like spoken street-level English rather than academic or museum language. Keep it short, immediate, recognizable, and slightly compressed. It may use a familiar Israeli or Hebrew term when the term helps recognition, but the sentence must make the meaning understandable to a visitor who does not already know it.

The front is a public cliché, cultural stereotype, self-parody, or outsider cliché. It is not presented as truth. Avoid degrading labels, hostile shorthand, and assumptions about intelligence, morality, criminality, loyalty, human worth, wealth, or inherent politics.

The `…BUT ACTUALLY,` side should remain plain English but shift into a calmer, more precise, evidence-led register. It should complicate the imagined picture rather than replace it with a flattering counter-stereotype. Evidence type and source metadata support the interpretation; they do not become the headline.

Approved register examples for terms include: `kippah srugah — a knitted kippah`, `Hiloni — secular`, `Masorti — traditional`, and `Novy God — the FSU-style New Year celebration`. Use such terms selectively and never require insider knowledge to understand the sentence.

These are mature enough to become project methodology:

1. **The nine-layer distinction.** Treatment, interaction atom, question atom, content form, guide move, guide stance, constraint, renderer, tour-level design. These never collapse.
2. **The authoring language.** BIG IDEA, OUTCOME, AHA, ANCHOR, TREATMENT, ASK, SHOW, DO, NOTICE, REVEAL, BRANCH, BRIDGE, SOURCE, CONSTRAINT, WAIT, NEXT. This is the vocabulary.
3. **The universal experience block** (envelope + treatment-specific payload). Every segment fills it.
4. **The three UX modes.** Author/prep, guide live, visitor/follow-up. Same underlying content, radically different rendering.
5. **The constraint set for this tour.** No simulation, no invented speech, graphic material opt-in, no causation assertion, contested numbers as ranges, testimony with consent. These are binding.
6. **Content form ≠ treatment.** A timeline is raw material. An interactive HTML timeline is a renderer. Chronology reconstruction is the treatment. This distinction is enforced everywhere.
7. **Questions are first-class.** They live inside treatments, not beside them. Every treatment specifies which question atoms fit and where.
8. **The branching table.** What the guide does with each type of answer. This makes questions real, not decorative.

## WHAT SHOULD REMAIN FLEXIBLE

These are professional judgment, not rules:

1. **Which treatment for which group.** No deterministic persona → treatment mapping. The guide decides based on stated purpose, demonstrated knowledge, questions asked, and observable attention.
2. **How many questions per stop.** No fixed number. No universal ratio of guide-talk to visitor-action.
3. **Treatment duration.** The time estimates are starting points, not prescriptions.
4. **Which renderer.** A guide with no tablet can deliver every treatment by voice and printed card. Digital is optional.
5. **Which combination recipe.** The grammar generates treatments. The library names known ones. The guide or designer can create new ones.
6. **Tone and register.** The guide adapts language to the group. The content slots provide material; the delivery is human.
7. **When to skip a block.** The guide reads the room. If energy is low, skip. If interest is high, extend. The pattern is a scaffold, not a cage.

## WHAT WE STILL NEED TO DECIDE BEFORE CODING

1. **Data model for content slots.** The treatment-specific payloads differ per treatment. Do we use a polymorphic schema (one table with a JSON payload column keyed by treatment type) or a typed schema (one table per treatment family)? The polymorphic approach is simpler to start; the typed approach enforces completeness. Decision needed.
2. **Guide live view rendering.** How minimal is minimal? The worked example shows SAY / ASK / SHOW / WAIT / REVEAL / NEXT as block types in a linear sequence. Is that enough, or does the guide need tabs for branches? Prototype needed.
3. **Visitor follow-up delivery.** Is it a link sent after the tour, a QR code at each stop, or an always-available tablet app? This affects how much content to prepare per segment.
4. **Testimony rights workflow.** The constraint says "written permission from archive and speaker." The system needs a rights-status field per testimony item with states: requested / granted / denied / withdrawn. Build it into the data model from day one.
5. **Multi-language.** The content is authored in English. Some sources are Hebrew. Visitors are English-speaking. Does the system hold both languages, or only the visitor-facing one? For source provenance, both are needed.
6. **Versioning.** Contested numbers change. New evidence appears. Testimonies may be withdrawn. The system needs versioned content items with audit trails. Design this before shipping.
7. **The remaining treatment cards.** The research identified 52 candidate treatments. This doc covers 22 across all 15 families. The remaining \~30 are variants and combinations. Decide whether to card them all or treat them as generated from the grammar.

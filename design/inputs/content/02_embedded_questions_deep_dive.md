## Core idea

A weak question creates fake participation: the guide asks something, but the answer has no effect on what happens next.

# Embedded questions as a reusable interaction system

**The design rule:** ask only when a visitor’s observation, reasoning, choice, or question can affect the next move. The guide might reveal a different piece of evidence, revisit a detail, invite a comparison, correct a claim, or carry the group’s question forward. A question followed by the same prepared speech regardless of the answer is participation in form, not in function. This is an adaptation of responsive museum facilitation and formative assessment—not a claim that a live tour is a classroom. [web:91][web:84]

The atoms below describe **work the visitor does**, not whether a prompt is open-ended or multiple-choice. A constrained choice can require substantial reasoning; an open-ended “Any thoughts?” can require almost none. All examples are **illustrative prompts for a hypothetical heritage site**, not claims about a particular site.

## Output 1 — Question-atom taxonomy

Evidence/practice labels: **L** = live heritage guiding or performance research; **M** = museum visitor research; **H** = historical-thinking or source-study practice; **C** = classroom research or teaching framework; **D** = digital/video study; **P** = professional guidance; **S** = design synthesis. A label identifies the *origin* of support, not proof that the atom works equally well in every setting.

| Question atom | Cognitive/interpretive purpose | Visitor action | Typical prompt form | Best moment | What may be withheld until response | Guide follow-up | Common failure mode | Evidence/practice type | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **NOTICE** | Establish shared, observable evidence. | Point to, describe, hear, or locate a detail. | “What do you notice at the edge of this photograph?” | First encounter with an object, place, image, sound, or video frame. | Caption or explanation. | Point to the named detail; invite one more observation before interpreting. | Asking for an interpretation before anyone has looked. | M; C; P | [web:16][web:54][web:122] |
| **PREDICT** | Make an expectation explicit so subsequent evidence can test it. | State an anticipated event or outcome. | “Given this opening, what do you expect the next account to describe?” | Just before the next story beat, demonstration, or source. | Outcome—not information needed to make a fair prediction. | Show the outcome; ask what confirmed or upset the expectation. | Treating an unknowable surprise as a knowledge test. | M; S | [web:91][web:98] |
| **INFER** | Move from detail to a bounded explanation. | Propose a conclusion and identify its basis. | “What might these wear marks suggest about use?” | After observation, before the guide supplies an interpretation. | One plausible interpretation or corroborating evidence. | Ask which mark supports it; distinguish inference from established fact. | Presenting a plausible guess as proven history. | H; C; P | [web:16][web:54][web:47] |
| **COMPARE** | Detect change, continuity, or meaningful difference. | Identify a specific similarity or contrast. | “What remains in the same position on these two maps?” | After both items are visible or recalled. | The guide’s summary of the difference. | Ask why the difference matters—and whether the sources are comparable. | Comparing items at incompatible scales or dates without warning. | H; M | [web:47][web:54][web:122] |
| **CHOOSE** | Prioritize among defensible options. | Select an explanation, route of inquiry, or evidence item. | “Which of these two sources would you examine first to check that claim?” | At a genuine interpretive fork. | The guide’s preferred answer or next evidence item. | Follow the chosen line briefly; explain what the other option could add. | Offering choices that cannot change the discussion. | P; C; S | [web:61][web:80] |
| **SEQUENCE** | Reconstruct temporal or causal order. | Put events, images, or decisions in order. | “Which had to happen before this building could operate?” | After enough events have been introduced. | The completed timeline. | Supply a dated anchor; distinguish documented order from inferred causation. | Making chronology into a speed-memory quiz. | H; S | [web:47][web:54] |
| **RETRIEVE** | Reactivate something encountered earlier for use now. | Recall a clue, claim, or unresolved question. | “What did we see at the gate that helps us read this ledger?” | At a later stop with a meaningful connection. | The previously discussed clue, briefly. | Restate it if needed, then apply it to the present evidence. | Testing an obscure name or date that has no interpretive payoff. | C; S | [web:31][web:43] |
| **CHALLENGE** | Make an interpretation open to disconfirmation. | Specify evidence that would change a claim. | “What finding would make you reconsider that explanation?” | After a claim has been stated. | A conflicting record or counterexample. | Present it if available; ask whether the claim needs revision. | An adversarial “gotcha” or implying every claim is equally uncertain. | H; C | [web:47][web:84] |
| **SOURCE CHECK** | Establish what a source can credibly tell us. | Identify creator, date, purpose, audience, or firsthand access. | “Who made this account, and what could they have witnessed?” | Before relying on testimony, a quotation, statistic, or document. | Authorship or production context, if discoverable from the item. | Add context; separate firsthand report from reported belief or hearsay. | Equating a source’s perspective with worthlessness. | H; P | [web:47][web:130][web:122] |
| **UNCERTAINTY** | Mark the boundary between evidence and conjecture. | Name what the available material cannot settle. | “What can this photograph *not* tell us about that day?” | After an inference or source reading. | Other evidence—or the fact that none survives. | State the strongest warranted claim and the remaining unknown. | Turning missing evidence into permission to invent. | H; P | [web:47][web:54][web:122] |
| **BRIDGE** | Give the next stop a question to answer or complicate. | Carry forward a specific puzzle. | “Which question should we take to the next building?” | At the close of a stop or during a transition. | The next stop’s relevant evidence. | Explicitly return to the question on arrival. | Asking for questions and never revisiting them. | M; P; S | [web:98][web:61] |
| **COMMIT** | Give a visitor’s initial reasoning something to be tested against. | Make a provisional selection or position. | “Before I turn the image over, which use seems most likely?” | Immediately before a fair reveal. | Reverse, label, second image, or result. | Reveal; invite comparison rather than announce winners. | Forcing public commitment on a sensitive matter. | C; S | [web:80][web:84] |
| **JUSTIFY** | Connect a claim to a visible detail or stated reason. | Point to supporting evidence. | “What do you see that makes you say that?” | Following a prediction, inference, or choice. | The guide’s reasoning. | Revoice the claim and evidence; seek an alternative reading if useful. | Repeating “why?” until it feels like cross-examination. | M; C | [web:19][web:16][web:122] |
| **REVISE** | Update an interpretation when new evidence appears. | Retain, qualify, or change an earlier answer. | “Now that we have the second account, what would you change?” | Immediately after a reveal or source comparison. | The guide’s synthesis. | Make the revision—and the reason for it—audible. | Rewarding only changed answers; retaining a supported answer also counts. | H; C | [web:54][web:80] |
| **CORROBORATE** | Judge where independent accounts converge or diverge. | Compare claims across sources. | “Which detail appears in both accounts, and which appears in only one?” | Once two sources are available. | A third source or the guide’s assessment. | Check whether the accounts are genuinely independent. | Treating repetition as independent confirmation. | H | [web:47][web:54] |
| **CONTEXTUALIZE** | Read a claim in its time, place, and circumstances. | Relate wording or action to conditions of production. | “How might the intended audience affect this wording?” | Before or after a striking quotation. | Relevant historical background. | Supply only the context needed to retest the reading. | Asking visitors to guess an unfamiliar historical context. | H | [web:47][web:122] |
| **TEST** | Turn an answerable puzzle into a small investigation. | Specify what to inspect, compare, or try. | “What could we check here to distinguish those explanations?” | When accessible evidence could resolve part of a question. | The result of the check. | Carry out the check; interpret what it does and does not show. | Asking a question the available object or site cannot answer. | M; S | [web:98][web:99] |
| **READ THE REPRESENTATION** | Distinguish a depiction from the historical thing depicted. | Identify a map key, image crop, reconstruction choice, scale, or category. | “What has this reconstruction had to fill in?” | Before treating a visualization as evidence. | Production notes or alternative reconstruction. | Label documented elements and interpretive additions. | Presenting a reconstruction or infographic as a transparent window onto the past. | H; P; S | [web:47][web:54] |
| **QUANTIFY / SCALE** | Give a number a meaningful denominator or comparison. | Estimate, calculate, or compare magnitudes. | “Is that total per day or across the whole year—and how would that change your reading?” | When a statistic or spatial scale enters the story. | Denominator, unit, or comparison figure. | Supply units and provenance; revisit the initial impression. | Using a dramatic number without units, source, or context. | H; S | [web:47][web:54] |
| **PERSPECTIVE CHECK** | Notice whose experience a claim describes and whose it omits. | Identify a bounded viewpoint or missing voice. | “Whose view of the street does this diary give us?” | With testimony, life stories, quotations, and institutional records. | A contrasting account, if one exists. | Preserve the speaker’s perspective; introduce other evidence without making the speaker stand for everyone. | Asking visitors to impersonate a historical person or speak for a group. | H; P; L | [web:122][web:61][web:152] |
| **GENERATE** | Produce a visitor-owned, investigable question. | Formulate what they want to find out and how. | “What question could this object actually help us investigate?” | After some unhurried observation. | The guide’s planned question. | Select or adapt a question that the available evidence can address. | Soliciting curiosity when there is no time or means to pursue any of it. | M; C | [web:98][web:54] |

**Withholding is conditional.** Delay an answer only when visitors have enough accessible evidence to do worthwhile work; do not conceal essential context, accessibility information, distressing content warnings, or the source status of an image. Project Zero’s observation routine, for example, separates what is seen from what is inferred, while Library of Congress guidance allows movement back and forth among observing, reflecting, and questioning rather than mandating a rigid sequence. [web:16][web:54]

| Placement | Function | Example | What it changes in the guide's next move | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Before explanation** | Surface a detail or initial model before naming its meaning. | “What looks altered on this doorway?” | Point to visitors’ detail first, or direct attention to an overlooked one; then explain. | An answer requiring context visitors have not received. [web:16][web:91] |
| **During a story** | Interrupt the narrative at a consequential decision, not an arbitrary sentence. | “With the crossing blocked, what options does the account suggest were available?” | Follow the option named into the next documented beat; distinguish documented options from guesses. | Breaking narrative momentum or turning historical hardship into entertainment. [web:152][web:47] |
| **Before a reveal** | Make an expectation testable. | “Which building do you think this cropped image shows?” | Uncrop or identify it; compare the decisive clue with the group’s choice. | A trick reveal based on information no one could have noticed. [web:80][web:16] |
| **After viewing an object** | Move from observation to inference and limits. | “Which mark looks like use rather than decoration—and what else would we need to know?” | Examine the proposed mark, then provide provenance or a comparison object. | Stating an inferred use as certain. [web:54][web:16] |
| **After source comparison** | Test an interpretation against agreement and conflict. | “What do both accounts establish, and where do they part company?” | Synthesize shared ground; source the discrepancy or introduce a third account. | Treating two accounts as equally informed, or forcing agreement. [web:47][web:122] |
| **During transition between stops** | Turn travel time into a forward-looking thread. | “At the next stop, what trace of the vanished route should we look for?” | On arrival, orient the group to that trace or explain why none survives. | Losing the promised thread; posing a question when people need safe wayfinding. [web:61][web:70] |
| **After a committed choice** | Let a choice have an interpretive consequence. | “You chose the council record. | **After a misconception appears** | Locate the reasoning and supply discriminating evidence. | “Which part of this image suggests that date? Let’s compare it with the dated plan.” | Use a dated anchor and invite revision; then continue. | Public embarrassment, extended correction of a minor slip, or repeating a harmful falsehood without clear correction. [web:91][web:61] |

## Output 3 — Questions inside content forms

These are **fit-for-purpose combinations**, not claims that each combination has been experimentally tested. “What not to do” identifies a failure specific to the form. Source-study combinations draw particularly on the Library of Congress and historical-reading frameworks; observation combinations draw on museum and visible-thinking practice. [web:54][web:47][web:16][web:122]

| Content form | Question atoms that fit | Example question | Why it works | What not to do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Story / narrative** | PREDICT → COMMIT → REVISE; CHALLENGE | “What does the letter lead you to expect next—and does the next account support it?” | Makes a story beat an evidence check. | Interrupt every beat, or imply that a surprising outcome was predictable. |
| **Timeline** | SEQUENCE → COMPARE → INFER | “Which change came first, and does that establish that it caused the second?” | Separates order from explanation. | Treat adjacent dates as proof of causation. |
| **Map** | NOTICE → READ THE REPRESENTATION → COMPARE | “Which path appears on the older map but not the new one, and what does the key tell us?” | Pairs spatial discovery with map literacy. | Ignore differing scales, boundaries, conventions, or purposes. |
| **Photograph** | NOTICE → INFER → UNCERTAINTY | “What is visible outside the doorway; what can’t this frame tell us?” | Keeps close looking separate from imagined surroundings. | Read beyond the crop or infer motives from a pose. |
| **Object** | NOTICE → TEST → INFER | “Which surface might show repeated handling, and what comparison would help us check?” | Turns a feature into a bounded investigation. | Assume wear has only one cause. |
| **Testimony** | SOURCE CHECK → PERSPECTIVE CHECK → CORROBORATE | “What did this speaker say they witnessed, and what are they reporting secondhand?” | Respects the account while distinguishing kinds of claims. | Dismiss testimony merely because it is subjective, or treat one recollection as universal. [web:122] |
| **Life story** | SEQUENCE → CONTEXTUALIZE → PERSPECTIVE CHECK | “Which choice does this biography document, and which constraints does it leave out?” | Connects an individual record to circumstances without claiming full access to a person’s mind. | Ask visitors to pretend they know exactly how the person felt. |
| **Quotation / dramatic reading** | NOTICE → SOURCE CHECK → REVISE | “Which word stands out when you hear it—and does knowing the intended audience alter your reading?” | Gives a reading a specific textual and historical task. | Use an unattributed excerpt as self-explanatory proof. [web:47][web:152] |
| **Statistic** | QUANTIFY / SCALE → SOURCE CHECK → COMPARE | “What population and period does this figure cover, and what is the appropriate comparison?” | Makes units and provenance part of interpretation. | Substitute a dramatic number for an argument. |
| **Primary source** | SOURCE CHECK → JUSTIFY → CORROBORATE → UNCERTAINTY | “Who made this, which phrase supports your claim, and what second source would you seek?” | Enacts sourcing, close reading, and corroboration. | Ask only “Is it biased?” and stop there. [web:47][web:54] |
| **Reconstruction** | READ THE REPRESENTATION → CHALLENGE → UNCERTAINTY | “Which parts are evidenced, and which would change if a new foundation were found?” | Exposes documented versus proposed elements. | Present an artist’s decision as an excavated fact. |
| **Infographic / visualization** | READ THE REPRESENTATION → QUANTIFY / SCALE → CHOOSE | “What does each symbol count, and would another denominator change which area looks largest?” | Makes the display’s design consequential. | Ask for a pattern before establishing units and categories. |
| **Video / digital media** | PREDICT → COMMIT → REVISE; NOTICE | “Pause here: which detail do you expect the next frame to clarify?” | A pause gives a specific viewing task and a subsequent check. | Pause merely to solicit a click or repeat the narration. [web:42] |
| **Landscape** | NOTICE → COMPARE → BRIDGE | “Which line in the ground might continue toward our next stop?” | Uses the present place to guide later observation. | Present an indistinct feature as definitively historical. |
| **Absence / vanished feature** | NOTICE → SOURCE CHECK → UNCERTAINTY | “What trace, if any, remains here—and what record tells us what once stood here?” | Treats absence as a question requiring evidence, not as evidence by itself. | Claim that an empty space proves a particular past use. |
| **Drive narration** | PREDICT → NOTICE → BRIDGE; RETRIEVE | “After the bend, look for a change in the roofline; what earlier map might help explain it?” | Gives a brief, forward-facing task that can be completed without conversation. | Demand searching, reading, voting, or prolonged discussion when occupants need to attend to travel and safety. |

## Output 4 — Responsive branching

Branch on the **answer’s evidential status and the group’s expressed interest**, not on assumed visitor traits. The guide can also decide that the prepared next step remains appropriate—but should make clear how the response informed that decision. Responsive museum facilitation includes observing, offering a fitting challenge or explanation, and sometimes hanging back; facilitation can also displace conversation among family members if the guide becomes its center. [web:91][web:84]

| Visitor response | Appropriate guide move | What not to infer | Example continuation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Correct, with supporting evidence** | Confirm the evidence; advance to a harder implication or contrasting source. | That everyone agrees or needs more difficulty. | “Yes—the dated entry supports that. Does the photograph show the same arrangement?” |
| **Correct, without a stated reason** | Invite a light-touch justification before moving on. | That the answer was a guess or fully understood. | “That fits. Which clue helped you decide?” |
| **Plausible but unsupported** | Revoice as a hypothesis; ask what could test it. | That a plausible answer is a fact—or that its speaker is uninformed. | “That could explain the marks. What would we expect to see elsewhere if it were true?” |
| **Misconception or inaccurate claim** | Identify the particular claim; provide a clear, relevant correction and evidence; offer a chance to revise. | A motive, identity, or fixed belief from one statement. | “The dated plan places the extension later. With that date, how would you adjust the sequence?” |
| **Disagreement within the group** | Ask each side for its strongest evidence; compare sources or state what remains open. | That disagreement needs a vote, or that both positions have equal evidential support. | “You are reading different parts of the map. Let’s check its key before deciding what either line means.” |
| **Silence** | Allow thinking time; shift to an accessible detail, a pair exchange, or an invitation to pass; then continue. | Disinterest, lack of knowledge, or a desire to be singled out. | “Take another moment. You can just point to one detail—or I’ll point out the one I’m curious about.” |
| **Unexpectedly sophisticated answer** | Acknowledge the distinction; invite a concise explanation or a harder counterexample, then reconnect the group. | That everyone wants an extended expert discussion. | “That distinction between when it was written and what it describes matters. Which date should we mark first?” |
| **Unexpected interest or visitor-generated question** | Follow it briefly if evidence and time permit; otherwise carry it to a specified stop or give an honest boundary. | That one person’s interest represents the whole group. | “The next room has a record that bears on that. Let’s hold your question and test it there.” |

Waiting, revoicing, inviting observations, and following visitors’ contributions appear in a study of selected strong museum-facilitation interactions; NPS dialogue guidance also offers pair-share and ways to participate without requiring everyone to speak aloud. Neither source makes silence a diagnosis. [web:122][web:61]

[web:152] | Questions inside dramatic readings need a clear participation invitation, an opt-out, and a return to sources and site. | Four cases do not identify an optimal question sequence or demonstrate that forced participation is beneficial. |
| **Historical-thinking classroom research** | A six-month quasi-experimental *Reading Like a Historian* intervention with **236 eleventh-grade students in five San Francisco high schools** found advantages on measured historical-thinking and other outcomes. [web:136][web:47] | Provides a disciplined account of the historical work that source questions can request. | Extended, taught document study is not evidence that a brief tour prompt will produce the same gains. |

## Output 6 — High-value reusable recipes

The arrows mark a **visitor task followed by a guide response or new task**. “REVEAL” means disclose relevant evidence and examine it, not stage a trick. “CONNECT” means apply an earlier idea to the present stop. These are synthesis recipes grounded in the observation, inquiry, historical-source, and responsive-facilitation practices above—not individually tested interventions. [web:16][web:98][web:47][web:91]

| Question recipe | Experience it creates | Where useful |
|---|---|---|
| **NOTICE → COMMIT → REVEAL** | A visible clue supports a provisional answer, then a caption or reverse tests it. | Photographs, objects, cropped images. |
| **NOTICE → INFER → JUSTIFY** | Visitors build an interpretation from a detail they can point to. | Objects, landscapes, artwork, photographs. |
| **NOTICE → UNCERTAINTY → REVEAL** | Visitors identify a genuine limit before a new source supplies some context. | Vanished features, ambiguous objects, photographs. |
| **PREDICT → SHOW SOURCE → REVISE** | The next document changes—or strengthens—an expectation for a stated reason. | Stories, testimony, timelines, video pauses. |
| **PREDICT → SHOW OUTCOME → CHALLENGE** | An outcome becomes an occasion to test the original explanation. | Guided discovery, demonstrations, narrative turns. |
| **COMPARE → INFER → SOURCE CHECK** | An apparent change becomes a historical claim whose sources must be examined. | Maps, paired photographs, before-and-after views. |
| **COMPARE → CORROBORATE → UNCERTAINTY** | Visitors distinguish shared evidence, conflicting claims, and the unresolved remainder. | Two accounts of an event, source packets, testimony. |
| **SOURCE CHECK → QUOTE → CONTEXTUALIZE** | A striking line acquires an author, audience, and historical setting. | Dramatic readings, quotations, letters. |
| **SOURCE CHECK → PERSPECTIVE CHECK → CORROBORATE** | Visitors respect a witness’s account without making it the whole story. | Oral history, life stories, institutional records. |
| **CHOOSE → JUSTIFY → CHALLENGE** | A genuine selection survives or fails a pertinent counterexample. | Competing explanations, which-source-first moments. |
| **COMMIT → DISCUSS → REVISE** | Different initial readings become an evidence-based group exchange. | Maps, reconstructions, short source comparisons. |
| **SEQUENCE → ANCHOR DATE → REVISE** | A timeline is repaired with a documented constraint rather than a correction-by-authority. | Construction phases, migrations, policy changes. |
| **RETRIEVE → CONNECT → BRIDGE** | An earlier clue explains part of this stop and raises a precise question for the next. | Multi-stop walks, drive narration. |
| **GENERATE → SELECT TESTABLE QUESTION → INVESTIGATE** | Visitor curiosity determines a small inquiry the available evidence can sustain. | Interactive objects, accessible archives, paired displays. |
| **NOTICE → TEST → INTERPRET RESULT** | Observation leads to a check and a bounded conclusion. | Material details, visible building phases, manipulable exhibits. |
| **READ THE REPRESENTATION → COMPARE ALTERNATIVE → REVISE** | Visitors recognize that a display makes choices, then test their effect. | Reconstructions, maps, infographics. |
| **QUANTIFY → SUPPLY DENOMINATOR → REASSESS** | A dramatic figure becomes a properly scaled historical claim. | Statistics, migration or production charts. |
| **BRIDGE → FIND TRACE → NAME LIMIT** | A promised search at the next stop pays off—even if the trace is absent. | Landscapes, vanished buildings, transitions. |

### What the evidence supports

Questions can support *observable inquiry work* when visitors have something to inspect or test and a chance to interpret the result. Museum studies support responsive facilitation while also showing a trade-off: a helpful guide can become the center of conversation at the expense of visitor-to-visitor talk. A heritage walking-tour study further supports treating question framing as part of the whole encounter, rather than as an isolated wording trick. [web:98][web:91][web:92]

### What is plausible professional practice

Plan each embedded question with four commitments: **the evidence visitors can use; the response the guide will listen for; at least two defensible next moves; and a way to close or carry forward an unresolved question**. Make participation invitational, especially in performance and difficult history. Attribute testimony, distinguish observation from inference, and say plainly when the surviving evidence does not settle a question. [web:84][web:152][web:122][web:54]

### What the evidence does not establish

It does not establish an optimal number of questions per stop, a universally superior atom or recipe, or that every question should delay an explanation. Nor do classroom retrieval effects, digital question-attempt rates, and science-museum inquiry results automatically establish memory or learning gains on live heritage tours. A question’s success must be judged by what visitors actually **notice, reason about, revise, or investigate—and what the guide does with it**. [web:31][web:42][web:98][web:84]

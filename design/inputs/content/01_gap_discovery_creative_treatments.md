## Core Principle

The guide knows the site and knows the people standing in front of them. From those two inputs, they set the educational target for this stop for this group. The creative treatment is how they deliver that target. Everything else stays in the drawer.

**Audience → Educational target → Creative treatment**

Treatments are tagged by group type, but the principle holds across all ages: creative guiding for people in their 20s works better for people in their 70s than no creativity at all. Invest more creativity in younger groups, but never default to lecture for older ones.

Tilden's line: the chief aim of interpretation is not instruction, but provocation. Relate it to the visitor's personality or experience, or it will not interest them. Interpretation for children must follow a fundamentally different approach, not a diluted adult version.

---

## Treatment Selection Rule

A treatment qualifies only if three inputs all say yes:

**Site nature** constrains which treatments are physically possible (open field vs. building, memorial vs. lookout, noise level, shade).

**Educational target** constrains which treatments serve the goal for this group at this stop.

**Audience** constrains which treatments will land (age, energy, language, group size, emotional state, religious observance, weather).

If any input says no, the treatment stays in the drawer.

## Treatments from תגלית Folder

### Parallel voices [TAG-rabin]
Four people experience the same event from different positions. The visitor finds the voice closest to theirs. No voice is declared right. Closes with a question, not an answer. **Groups:** all ages. For adults, reduce to 2–3 voices read aloud by the guide. For teens/20s, assign voices to participants.

### Sensory mapping [TAG-havdalah]
Map an idea to the five senses in 60 seconds. Taste, smell, sight, touch, hearing — each tied to a concrete object or action. **Groups:** all ages. Works as a micro-moment at any stop. At the Oct 7 tour: the wind, the smell of the field, the silence, the texture of a memorial wall, the sound of a siren countdown.

### Embedded provocative questions [TAG-masada]
Pause the narrative at a decision point and ask the visitor a question they can answer from what they see. "Why was a sauna required in a desert?" is a model: it uses the site itself as the evidence. Never ask "how would you feel?" — ask "what do you see?" or "why here?" **Groups:** all ages. For older adults, ask fewer questions but make each one count. For teens, increase frequency.

### Four corners / floor scale [TAG-methods]
A dilemma, four positions (or a spectrum on the floor), visitors walk to their position. Forces physical commitment to a stance. **Groups:** teens and 20s primarily. For older adults, adapt as a seated show-of-hands or a card hold-up. **Limit:** never at a memorial stop. Use only at the Tel Aviv framing session or on the bus.

### Aquarium debate [TAG-methods]
Two people argue assigned positions (not their own). Others watch, then tap in. Teaches perspective-taking without simulation. **Groups:** teens and 20s. For adults, the guide can run it as a "what Side A would say is..." narration instead. **Limit:** positions must be factual and sourced, not invented.

### What comes first / timeline cards [TAG-methods]
Events on cards, visitors order them chronologically. Reveals assumptions. **Groups:** all ages. For the Oct 7 tour: use the long-view timeline (1948–2023) at Stage 1. Hand out 8–10 cards. The ordering itself is the learning.

### Card sort / ranking [TAG-methods, TAG-identity]
Values, words or images on cards, visitors rank by importance or discard by elimination. Externalizes priorities without lecture. **Groups:** all ages. For the Oct 7 tour: "what makes a community?" using words drawn from the kibbutzim.

### Contested text study [TAG-wow]
Two opposing texts on the same issue, read side by side. Questions: define the problem, which side do you identify with, does this concern you personally. **Groups:** adults and older teens. Requires reading level and patience. **Oct 7 application:** pre-2023 Israeli political division, two op-eds.

### Slam poem / spoken word [TAG-identity]
A poem that names the contradictions of identity, read aloud, then discussed. "Which line do you identify with?" **Groups:** teens and 20s. For older adults, replace with a short personal testimony clip that does the same thing — names contradictions.

### The beauty acknowledgment [BRAINSTORM]
"This place is beautiful. That is part of the story." Pause and let the landscape speak. Do not explain why it matters. **Groups:** all ages. Use after the heaviest stop, or at the start when arriving at the envelope and seeing the fields for the first time.

### "What he can't do anymore" — naming the unlived [TAG-herzl, Beaufort / Ron Leshem]
A list of things the dead person will never do. Not heroic, not sentimental — specific and ordinary: rent an apartment, run for doughnuts in the rain, take a kid to a movie. The power is in the everyday. **Groups:** 20s and 30s primarily (the language is raw, young, Israeli soldier register). For older adults, the guide can read one paragraph and let it sit. For the Oct 7 tour: do not write this list yourself. Use it only if a survivor or family member has published one and cleared it. **Limit:** never improvise this for a victim. The treatment works because it was written by someone who knew them.

### Yizkor ceremony structure [TAG-herzl]
A memorial micro-ceremony: Yizkor prayer, El Maleh Rachamim, Hatikva. Bilingual (Hebrew + English). **Groups:** Jewish-identified groups, all ages. For the Oct 7 tour: use only at the closing moment, if the group wants it. Never impose. Offer it as an option alongside secular alternatives (a moment of silence, a view, a name reading).

### Life story of one fallen person [TAG-herzl, Reuven Politi]
Read the official Yizkor memorial page of one person: born here, studied there, loved this, fell at age 19. The specificity is the treatment — not "soldiers died" but "Reuven loved art, wrestling, and horse riding, and was killed by Syrian artillery at Tel Antar on his 19th birthday." **Groups:** all ages. For the Oct 7 tour: select one person per stop whose story fits the site and the group. Source from official memorial databases (izkor.gov.il) with attribution.

### Kamtza and Bar Kamtza — internal division destroys from within [TAG-guides]
The Talmudic story where a party invitation mix-up, public humiliation and rabbinic inaction lead to the destruction of the Temple. The moral: internal hatred (sinat chinam) brings down what external enemies could not. **Groups:** Jewish-identified, all ages. Especially effective for the Tel Aviv framing session when introducing the 2023 internal division. **Limit:** present it as a parallel the tradition itself draws, not as the guide's political argument. Let the group make the connection.

### Station-based group work with primary sources [TAG-rabin lesson plan]
Divide the group into small teams, each gets a different primary source (a quote, a document, a photo). Each team analyzes their source and presents to the whole group. The guide synthesizes. **Groups:** teens and 20s primarily. For older adults in small groups (2–4), run it as a guided reading of 2–3 sources in sequence. For the Oct 7 tour: use at the Tel Aviv framing session with pre-2023 sources (op-eds, photos, Knesset quotes).

### The educator's own honest uncertainty [TAG-rabin lesson plan]
Rabin's lesson plan closes with: "These are hard questions. I don't have clean answers." The guide models honest uncertainty rather than performing authority. **Groups:** all ages. This is not a technique to deploy — it is a stance. The guide says "I don't know" or "sources disagree" when that is true.

## Treatments from Research Sources

### Provocation not instruction [TILDEN]
The guide's job is to provoke the visitor to think, not to deliver a lecture. Ask a question that connects the site to the visitor's own experience. **Groups:** all ages.

### Open forum: raise both sides [ICSC]
A site of conscience raises both sides of an issue. Individual real people make hard topics discussable at human scale. Distance from the present enables conversation. **Groups:** adults. For the Tel Aviv framing session: present the 2023 division as two positions, not one narrative.

### No simulations [USHMM]
Never ask visitors to pretend they are victims, attackers, or bystanders. Simulations trivialize the experience. The way to build empathy is testimony, not role-play. **Groups:** all ages. This is a hard rule for the Oct 7 tour.

### Verbatim quotes only [OHA]
Any direct speech in the tour must be verbatim, from a named speaker, with source and rights. Reconstructed dialogue of victims is invented content. **Groups:** all ages.

### Graphic material is opt-in [USHMM]
Graphic images and text are never the default. The visitor chooses to see them. The guide describes what exists without showing it. **Groups:** all ages. Required for the Oct 7 tour.

### The guide carries secondary trauma [FEG, Leshem 2018]
Guides at dark sites accumulate psychological stress. Build in debrief, rotation, and self-care. This is not a treatment for visitors but a design constraint for the guide.

## Treatments from Brainstorm Session

### Directed looking
"Stand here. Look south. What do you see?" before any narration. Let the site speak first. **Groups:** all ages.

### Sound and silence
Let ambient sound carry the moment: wind, birds, the absence of sound, a siren test. At Sderot: count the 15-second warning in your head. **Groups:** all ages.

### Scale and distance
Use the visitor's body to feel a number. "The fence was 800 meters. That building is 800 meters." **Groups:** all ages. Strongest for first-time visitors who have no physical reference.

### Absence
Point at what is not there. The empty slab where a house stood. The gap in a row of trees. Pair with a before-photo on the tool. **Groups:** all ages.

### Single-person thread
Follow one named person through this stop's timeline slot. Requires a cleared testimony and a story that fits the site. **Groups:** all ages. For adults, the guide narrates. For teens, play a 2-minute clip.

### Parallel lives (drive narration)
"At 06:29, here they were doing X. Three kilometers away, they were doing Y." Use the drive between stops to bridge the timeline. **Groups:** all ages.

### Reversal
Start with the outcome, then walk backward to the cause. Use when the site shows aftermath and the visitor needs to reconstruct. **Groups:** adults primarily.

### Unanswered question
Open with a question, give the stop's content, do not answer it. Let it sit. **Groups:** all ages. Use when the educational target is judgment or moral complexity.

### Map reading
Hand them a before/after map. "Find this community. Now look at the 2024 map." Maps are evidence, not games. **Groups:** all ages.

### Choice and opt-in
"You can listen to a 2-minute testimony clip here, or walk the perimeter in silence. We meet at the bus in 5." Both paths are equal. **Groups:** all ages. Required at any stop with graphic or heavy material.

### Landscape narration on the drive
Use the drive for context, timeline bridging, lighter material. The van is relief space. **Groups:** all ages.

### Food with purpose
Lunch after the heaviest stop. Choose a place with life in it. **Groups:** all ages.

### Closing ritual
End the day with something the visitor does, not something they hear. A view, a toast, a moment of quiet, a drive back in silence with music. **Groups:** all ages.

## Ethical Limits on Treatments

These apply to all groups at all stops on the Oct 7 tour.

**No simulation.** Never ask a visitor to imagine being a victim, to hide in a shelter, to run, or to act out any part of the event.

**No invented speech.** Every quote attributed to a real person must be verbatim and sourced. The PAST framework allows "words that could have been used" — that is not acceptable here.

**Silence belongs to the visitor.** The guide may create a pause, but the silence is not a performance tool. It is space the visitor owns.

**Graphic material is always opt-in.** The default is non-graphic. The visitor actively chooses to see or hear difficult material. Both paths (opt-in and opt-out) are equal.

**No humor at memorial stops.** Humor belongs on the drive, at lunch, in the guide's own humanity. Never about the events or the people.

**No "how would you feel" questions.** Ask what they see, what they notice, why this place, why this time. Never ask them to simulate a victim's emotion.

**Testimony requires consent and rights.** Any clip played on the tour must have written permission from the archive and, through it, from the speaker. A withdrawal procedure must exist.

**Contested numbers are presented as ranges.** Name the sources, give the range, move on. The guide does not pick a side on disputed figures.

**The tour asserts no causation between the 2023 internal division and Oct 7.** It presents the division as context, not as cause.

**The largest gap in the attached inventory is not another way to tell a story. It is the set of structures in which visitors investigate evidence, test an explanation, make something, teach one another, or contribute a bounded piece of knowledge.** The catalogue below adds **52 candidate treatments** while preserving the inventory’s distinction between an experience structure and the material or medium used to deliver it.

**Scope.** These are candidates for a guide’s palette, not recommendations for every site. At a memorial or an October 7 stop, the attachment’s prohibitions on simulation, invented speech, compulsory disclosure, and default exposure to graphic material still govern. A treatment imported from drama or games does **not** override those constraints. ## Output 8 — Blind spots

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| **1. Which families are commonly overlooked by conventional tour-guide training?** | In this inventory’s terms: evidence investigation, material-process testing, peer teaching, authored visitor artifacts, co-curation, and trade-off deliberation. Conventional interpretation does include questions and demonstrations; the gap is to give visitors a *bounded consequential task* with them. NPS materials explicitly recognize demonstrations and activities, while archival and participatory-museum resources open less narration-centered formats. [web:25][web:28][web:92][web:129] |
| **2. Which outside fields plausibly transfer?** | Historical thinking, archival education, experimental archaeology, systems thinking, public deliberation, drama’s facilitation techniques, participatory art, sound art, participatory photography, and crowdsourcing. Transfer the **structure**, then re-check accuracy, accessibility, rights, and site ethics. [web:145][web:80][web:95][web:55][web:24][web:63][web:131] |
| **3. Which common-in-practice treatments appear poorly studied for this precise setting?** | Especially brief craft-demonstration pauses, adult clue hunts, visitor captions, sound-layer comparison, and guide-led rephotography. The sources here primarily establish actual formats and practitioner use, **not comparative effectiveness for live guides at sensitive heritage sites**. [web:26][web:5][web:70][web:78][web:115] |
| **4. Which child-associated formats could adapt for adults?** | Mystery bags, scavenger searches, sketch-from-memory, parts-and-purpose inquiry, and bounded puzzles—by changing the intellectual question and tone rather than merely making a children’s worksheet harder. Adult museum hunts and adult-facing experimental-archaeology programming show that play and handling need not imply a child audience. [web:140][web:5][web:77] |
| **5. Which require digital technology; which almost none?** | A specifically **AR** version of image alignment needs a device; a recorded sound layer or archival upload needs playback or a contribution channel. The underlying alignment, listening inquiry, caption, sketch, uncertainty inventory, claim–support–question close, and many peer exchanges do not require an app. The documented Streetmuseum AR overlay and Smithsonian online transcription are renderer/workflow examples, not reasons to conflate medium with treatment. [web:115][web:131][web:31] |
| **6. What important engagement forms were absent?** | Testing and revising explanations; corroborating independent evidence; solving a source-linked puzzle; learning a safe material skill; producing an accountable caption, sketch, or map; exchanging complementary findings; weighing multiple trade-offs; and leaving a reviewed contribution. These add **test, make, teach, deliberate, and contribute** to the inventory’s strong **notice, hear, compare, and discuss** repertoire. [web:138][web:80][web:10][web:55][web:129] |
| **7. What deserves a focused evidence-validation run later?** | Compare learning and visitor choice across guide-led inquiry versus narration; examine accuracy gains and misleading certainty in puzzles/AR; test adult accessibility and group-flow costs; study whether creative production strengthens observation; evaluate safeguarding and consent in testimony-based formats; assess whose knowledge participatory contributions preserve, who reviews them, and whether contributors receive follow-up. These are **validation questions**, not claims of demonstrated effects. Relevant existing formats and ethical facilitation guidance provide the starting cases. [web:1][web:117][web:16][web:131] |

**Generative rule:** specify the educational target and permissible evidence first; choose a **visitor-experience structure** next; select atoms that make visitors do the desired intellectual work; choose a renderer last. For example, **SOURCE → PREDICT → COMPARE → REVEAL → REVISE** can be run with a guide’s narration, two printed images, accessible tactile material, or a screen. Changing the screen does not create a new treatment.
